The world

Arena of Kits takes place on the Dial, a round land in an Outer Sea, laid out like a sundial. At its centre stands the Tower, which scholars call the Gnomon: the pin of the sundial. Once the sun walked the sky above it, and the Tower's shadow swept round the Dial and told every realm its hour.

The realms lie around the Tower like the hours on a dial. At the top, the white north of Frostfall, where the stars turn round the pole. In the east, Aubade on the morning cliffs, and below it the Sands. In the south-east, the Drowned Coast and the broken sky towers of the Spires standing in the sea. In the south, the Deep under the southern hills. In the south-west, the Field of Banners. In the west, the Walking Wood. In the north-west, Gloaming, where the light fails first.

The Dial as a sundial: the realms sit at their hours round the Tower. Wedges are the realms in their faction's colour; the dark line is the Tower's shadow, stopped at the hour of the Fall.

The Noonfall

A hundred years ago, at noon on a midsummer day, the sun stopped over the Tower, cracked, and fell. It fell because the Tower took it (## The Tower and the Climb). Its heart fell at the Tower's foot and still burns beneath it in the Hall of Noon: the Heart of Noon, the fallen sun. Its two last embers fell into the Cinder Pit, where they burn as Ash and Ember. Its keeper, the Sun Warden, stayed at his post in the Sun Court at the Tower's top, and his great dial still strikes.

Its heat lifted the sea over the Drowned Harbour. Its light ran down into the crystals of the Deep. The Sands say it fell into their dunes, and the sand is still warm. Where it had hung, the sky is torn: the Tear, black with a burning pink edge, and through it came the Unmaker.

A day still comes. Each morning a thinner light rises from the Heart through the Tower, and the realms live by it. But it is a little paler every year, the hours slip, and the weather keeps no appointments.

The slipping hours have a strange gift. Everyone who was alive on the day of the Fall, or born in the week after it, ages slowly, about a year for every five; the Hourkeepers call them the Noon-touched. That is why a dancer who was a girl on the day of the Fall, a boy who swept the Ring's first sand and a countess who saw the light leave her lands still walk the Dial a hundred years later, and why the young of the Dial listen to them about the sun. Everyone on the Dial wants something done about the sun: hang it again, keep it down, burn it, take it, or understand it first.

Why heroes come to the Ring

In the first years after the Fall the realms went to war over the sun's pieces. The war was fought on the Field of Banners, crimson against azure, until the field was mud and the king who began it was buried. It would have gone on until the Dial was ash. Instead the realms swore the Truce of the Ring: no realm makes war over the sun again; every quarrel is settled by champions, one against one, in the Ring at the Tower's foot; and the Tower's gate opens only for a champion of the Ring.

That is the Arena. Each duel settles a debt, moves a border, wins a right or answers an insult, and every win is a step toward the right to climb. Most heroes also bring a reason of their own, and a rival they would very much like to meet in the Ring. The Truce holds duels in the realms too, on fields it has blessed: the Drowned Harbour, the Verdant Glade, the Sandstone Canyon, the Crystal Cavern, the Fallen Battlefield, Frostfall Lake, the Night Temple, the Sky Ruins and the Ember Caldera.

The Tower and the Climb

The user's choice, 2026-11-15: the sun fell because the Tower took it, and the Tower is a collector of worlds. The canon in full: docs/lore/codex/canon.md; the whole story: docs/lore/codex/story/.

The Tower is a made thing. Its makers, on a world called Senmere whose sun was failing, called it the Aumbry, the keeping-place: it takes a thing at its hour and keeps it, as it was, for ever. It kept their world, then crossed the dark to the Dial and stood at its centre as its gnomon, older than any memory, waiting for the Dial's sun to age. Three hundred years before the Fall it began to draw on the sun at noon, and the sun paled; at noon on midsummer of year 0 it closed its hand and took the sun's noon, and the sun's body cracked in its grip and fell. Most of the Dial still does not know it.

Since the Fall the Tower has been taking the realms in. Its floors hold them as they were at the hour it took them: the Sea as the Drowned Harbour on the morning the water came, the Wood walking, the Sands and their storm, the Deep without light, and the War on the Field of Banners, where the two armies still fight because nobody inside has told them it is over. The people of the floors (the Bell-Warden, the Moss-Abbess, the Star-Reader, the Echo-Hermit, the Herald and the rest) were there when the Tower took them, and they still keep their posts. Each floor's ruler is the worst thing its realm had.

The crowns of the chapters are the sun's own: the Sun Warden on the tenth and fortieth floors, Ash and Ember on the twentieth and fiftieth, and the Heart of Noon itself on the thirtieth. Above the fiftieth floor is the Noon Seat, where the sun rested at noon. It looks empty from below: it is where the Tower keeps the noon it took. Whoever reaches it decides what hangs there.

Each chapter's gate is held by the Tower's echo of a hero of that realm, kept from a duel in the Ring: Marin at the Sea, Fenna at the Wood, Sefu at the Sands, Opaline at the Deep. At the War the gatekeeper is Oblivion, because that endless war is the one thing on the Dial it has truly unmade.

But a new sun would end them, and they will do a great deal to keep the Noon Seat empty. They keep the letter of the Truce and very little of its spirit.
