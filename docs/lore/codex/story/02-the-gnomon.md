# 2. The Gnomon

*Part one, the Age of the Shadow. The Dial grows up round the Tower and sets its clocks by it, and the Tower learns the Dial.* ·
[The Long Noon](README.md)

## The hand of the clock

For more than a thousand years of the Dial's own counting, the Tower's shadow was the most trusted thing in the world.

It swept round the land every day, from the white north in the morning to the night-side heights in the evening, and every realm knew
its hour by it. Markets opened when it touched the market stone. Courts sat while it lay across the court step. In Frostfall the
herders who would become the Hourkeepers built a ring of stones on the shore of the frozen lake to watch the stars turn round the Nail,
the one star that never moves, and within a century they were telling the hour from the shadow as well. In the east, a fishing village
on the cliffs learned to read the shadow across its bay, and a woman of that village, the story goes, stood on the cliff edge one dawn
and sang to the rising sun, and it came up faster. The village became Aubade, the city that rings the morning up. An oath sworn "in the
shadow" was sworn before the whole Dial, and nobody broke one.

Nobody asked what the shadow was for. It was a shadow. It was what the sun did with the Tower.

It was not. It was the Tower's hand. Wherever it passed, every day, the Tower learned that place a little better: the hours it kept,
the people in it, what they would weep for. Over a thousand years it learned the Dial the way a careful keeper learns a house before
the family moves out: every room, every drawer, which floorboard creaks. It was patient. The sun was young.

## The ones who came close

Some of the Dial's peoples came closer to the Tower than others, and some of them were the first things it kept.

The mole-folk had been under the southern hills longest. Their oldest seam-song, the Song of the First Lamp, already calls the Tower
**the pin above**, and already has a verse about a sound in the deepest rock "like roots drinking". The mole-folk did not dig toward
it. They have always known which way not to dig.

The Briarkin of the Walking Wood, which was then the Still Wood, kept their great revels in the glades, but once in every lifetime of a
tree each of their courts held a revel at the Tower's foot, at midsummer, in its shadow, for luck. About three hundred years before the
Fall it was the turn of the **Fox court**. They danced at the Tower's foot all one midsummer night, under masks, and in the morning, when
the shadow reached them, the revel was not there. The grass was flattened in a ring, and there were lanterns burning in the dew, and
nobody. The Tower had taken its hour. Among the dancers was a young showman of the court who could pull a flame out of a sleeve, and his
sister, a fox girl who stole other people's words for fun. The Fox court mourned them, and then, as the Briarkin do, it forgot them,
and in forgetting them it let their names fade from the world. Its oldest memory begins after that night.

The Coast folk built their Spires in the sea off the Drowned Coast, the tallest towers anyone on the Dial had made, in imitation, some
said, of the Tower itself. Rooms appeared in them over the years that nobody had built, and nobody could find the doors to. The Coast
folk walled them up. They did not know that the Tower's roots ran under the sea floor too, and that a root can open a room.

A ferryman's boy drowned in a river of the Wood four hundred years before the Fall, carrying the body of a girl across a flood in the
night; he got up again, as some of the dead of the Dial do, with his work unfinished, but her body was never found. He looked for it
for four hundred years. It is in the Tower. He is [Ostrel](../heroes/ostrel.md), and he would not learn this until the hundredth summer.

These were small keepings. The Tower was not hungry yet. It was learning.

## The courts of the Dial

The Dial grew up. About six hundred years before the Fall, at the oasis of Sefet, the first king of the Sands took the crook and called
the sun his father, and the **Dune Throne** began. Its kings were buried in the Valley of Lids with their hearts weighed against a
feather. The first king did something none of his heirs repeated: he walked across the Dial to the Tower's foot, and found its stair,
and climbed it. At the top, in a court of white stone with a great dial set into the floor, he found a man in gold and white keeping
a post nobody had given him. The king asked him what the Tower was. The man could not tell him; the word that bound him would not let
him. But he could walk with a guest, and he walked the king up one stair further, to a window, and let him look.

Through the window the king saw a country of terraces round a valley, under a great red sun that did not move, and people on every
terrace standing still and glad in the noon light, as if they had been standing so for ever. When he came down he never spoke of it. He
had it painted in his tomb, in the oldest glyphs, beside the warning he wrote under it, and he had the tomb sealed with a law: that it
was to be opened only if the Father ever began to fail. The warning said:

*The pin keeps suns. It kept one before ours. It will keep ours. Do not sing to it.*

Nobody sang to it for two hundred years. Then the Aubade folk did.

## The Hymn

About four hundred years before the Fall the man in gold and white came down the Tower's stair for the first time anyone remembers, and
walked east along what would become the Dawn Road, to Aubade, the city that sang to the sun.

He did not want to. The word made him. A keeping-place must learn the thing it will keep, and it learns best by being sung to, and the
Aumbry, standing over a young world with an ageing sun, had begun to want its song. Iselm, the **Sun Warden**, as Aubade came to call
him, taught the bell quarter's best singers a hymn in an old tongue none of them knew, to be sung at the Tower's top every noon by a
choir of a hundred. It was the song Senmere had sung, and its verses drew the sun to the Tower at noon. Aubade believed the Hymn held
the sun up in the sky. It held it, a little more every noon, to the Tower.

He taught them one thing more, which the word had not asked for. The Hymn had a last verse, to be sung only at midsummer, by the
choir's master alone: a verse of evening, of letting go. He had written it himself, long ago, for another sun. Each midsummer, when the
master sang it, the Tower let go of that year's noon, and the sun walked on. The Warden could not tell them why it mattered. He could only
tell the first master, in the plain words of the Ring-speech's grandmother, that the last verse must never be forgotten, and that each
master must teach it to the next before he died. For four hundred years every master did.

The choir climbed the Tower every morning. It became the great honour of Aubade, a place in the hundred. Families gave their best
voices to it. The singers came home every evening with the light still on their faces, and said that the Warden met them at the top as
if they were late, and that nobody had ever heard him laugh.

## The paling

About three hundred years before the Fall, the Hourkeepers of Frostfall measured, on the great lens of their observatory, that there was
a little less light at midsummer noon than there had been the year before.

It was the Tower beginning to draw. The sun's ten thousand years were not done, but the Aumbry had found the Dial's sun older than it
liked, and it had begun, at noon each day, through the Hymn's drawing verses, to take a sip. The Hourkeepers measured the same thing the
next year, and the next. They began the **Noon Folio**, a book that records the light at every midsummer, and they kept it secret,
because they were not sure, and the Hourkeepers do not say what they are not sure of. In three hundred years they were never sure. They
wrote, near the end, one line: *it is dying, and something hastens it.*

Other things began to change in those centuries, and nobody joined them up. In Gloaming the dead had got up in the deep crypts for
generations before the paling, wherever the crypts lay over one of the Tower's roots, and the Ossuary Pact had sealed them in; now they
got up more often. The things the Tower was learning to keep were things that end, and an ending is harder near a keeper. A scholar of
the dead named [Mortis](../heroes/mortis.md) died at his desk in Gloaming with a question unanswered, and got up three days later. A
clerk of the counting house of Harrow died sixty years before the Fall in the middle of a sum, and got up to finish it, and became
[Mr. Ledger](../heroes/mr-ledger.md). The first Ninefold knight swore her oath at the Keep of Nine on the Field, and the crimson kings of
Harrowmark grew rich on the road traffic to the Tower's foot, where a midsummer market and a wrestling circle of straw rope gathered the
whole Dial once a year.

The nine standing stones round the Tower's foot, with their four words in a tongue nobody could read, were knocked over, carted off,
built into walls, or buried by the market. One of them, a limestone pillar with a face some mason had carved into it long after, ended
up in the masons' halls of the Deep, where a mason's boy would one day call it Gerald.

## The tomb is opened

In the year before the Fall, the young queen of the Sands broke the law of the first king.

[Asenet](../heroes/asenet.md) had been crowned at twenty-five, the year her father died. She was a scholar of the tomb records, clever
and cold, and believed that a court should know what it was talking about. The Throne's priests had noticed, on the Glass Flats, what the Hourkeepers noticed on their lens: the
Father's warmth on the sand was less every midsummer. They said nothing, as priests do. Asenet heard them say nothing, and asked why, and
when nobody would answer she went to the Valley of Lids by night with two lamps and a pry-bar, and opened the first king's tomb, because the
law said it was to be opened if the Father began to fail, and the Father was failing.

She read the warning. She saw the painting of the country of terraces under the red sun that did not move. She understood more of it
than anyone on the Dial had understood in a thousand years, because she was the first to read it with the paling in her mind.

She tried. She told the court, and the court thought the young queen had gone strange in the tombs. She wrote to the Hourkeepers, who
wrote back politely that they had no measurements they could share. She rode to Aubade, against the advice of everyone, and asked to see
the master of the choir, and told him to his face: the pin keeps suns; do not sing to it. The master was an old man who had sung the
Hymn for fifty years. He did not believe her. But he was a careful man, and he could not forget her face, and when he climbed the Tower
the next morning the Warden, who could say nothing, looked at him for a long time over the great dial, and then said, as if remarking on
the weather:

"This year, teach it to another."

## The last week

The master chose the youngest soprano in the choir's history, a bell-ringer's daughter of twenty-six with the purest voice in Aubade,
who would one day be called [Mum](../heroes/mum.md). In the week before midsummer of the year the Dial would call year 0, he taught her the
last verse, which only he knew, in case, he told her, anything happened to him. She learned it in three days. She was to sing it with him,
for the first time in four hundred years in two voices, at noon on midsummer day.

She was proud, and frightened, and in love with the baritone at the master's other hand, a man called [Cantor](../heroes/cantor.md) who
sang everything on the beat and teased her for thinking too much. She thought about the last verse all that week. She was thinking about it
on midsummer morning, climbing the Tower's stair with the rest of the hundred, while the bells of Aubade rang the sun up behind them for
what would be the last time.

At the top, the Warden was waiting at his dial. When Mum came in, he turned his head, as he always did for her, and this time he did not
look away.
