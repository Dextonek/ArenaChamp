# 9. The Sands and the Deep

*Part four, the Climb, floors 21 to 40. A king meets the night he was crowned, a sun's heart is weighed, and the Tower's keeper shows
what it is.* · [The Long Noon](README.md)

## The night of the storm

The twenty-first floor was the Storm Canyon in the Sands on the night of the great storm of year 1: red walls, sand in the air thick
enough to chew, a beacon tower on the rim, caravan towns half buried, raiders crouched behind their shields waiting for a caravan that
would never come. The keeper of the Sands' floors is the **Star-Reader**, who climbs the beacon each night to read the sky through the
storm and has never once seen a star. The **Storm-Counter** counts the lightning and the **Beacon-Keeper** keeps the fire lit.

The gate was held by the Tower's echo of [Sefu](../heroes/sefu.md) on the night it took the canyon: twenty-four, the crown new on his
head, the sandstone crook in his hand, and afraid.

Sefu went to meet it himself. He was a hundred and twenty-four and looked forty-four, and he had worn the cursed crown for ninety-nine
years and outlasted everyone else it passed to, because his embalmer brewed him a draught each night that kept it from taking him and
shortened his life by a year for each month. He stood in front of himself in the storm, and the echo raised the crook to Judge him, and
Sefu did not raise his.

"I did not take it," he said, to the boy with his face. He said it loud enough for the whole company to hear, and for his sister. "They
offered it at midnight, and I was afraid of what they would do if I said no. I have said for a hundred years that I took it."

The echo lowered the crook. It had been kept at the hour of a lie, and the lie was over. It stood aside. [Asenet](../heroes/asenet.md)
had heard. She did not say anything. But she walked beside her brother through the storm after that, and he let her.

## The worst of the storm

The Sands' rulers were old, and some of them were older than the Fall. The **Scarab Colossus** of the twenty-first floor, a beetle the size
of a house that the storm had uncovered under the canyon, burrowed and marked the sand and called the storm; the Dune folk say it rolled the
sun across the sky once, and the mother of a god had a right to be angry that the sun was gone. Her son, the gilded **Scarab King**,
worshipped by the raiders, ruled the twenty-second. The **Dune Colossus** walked with the dunes. The **Brass Sphinx** of the twenty-fourth
asked her riddle, which nobody had ever answered: *what holds the noon and has no hands?* Asenet answered it. "The pin," she said, "and it
has one." The Sphinx was so surprised she forgot to fight.

The **Glass Serpent**, born the day the sun's heat struck the sand, ruled the twenty-fifth. On the twenty-sixth was the **Mirage Lord**, a
prince of the Throne who had walked onto the Glass Flats to be alone and become a mirage of himself; the Throne's archer of the mirage,
[Sahar](../heroes/sahar.md), whose grandmother's brother he was, made him a mirage in return, of a road home across the Flats with a lamp at
the end of it, and the Mirage Lord walked onto it, and lay down in the shade of a palm that was not there, and was quiet. The **Sand Tyrant**,
who had sold water by the cup in a drought, the **Red Jackal**, who had ridden for the crimson, and the **Sun-Eater**, a priest of the Father
who had tried to swallow a piece of the fallen sun to become it and burned from inside, ruled the floors above. The Throne's lion of the gate,
[Ramet](../heroes/ramet.md), on the stone lion that is not stone, carried the company through the worst of the storm. The Throne's embalmer,
[Vitriol](../heroes/vitriol.md), took samples of everything, in small glass vials, and labelled them, and was happier than anyone had seen
him in two hundred years.

## The Father's heart

At the thirtieth floor, in a hall of black glass, hung **the Heart of Noon**: the Tower's keeping of the sun's heart in the instant it fell, a
sphere of white fire whose light was the day. It was the closest any of them had come to the thing that had been taken.

It fought them, as the crowns do. It did not want to; it could not be other than it had been in that instant, which was falling, and
furious, and afraid. When it was brought down it hung low over the black glass floor, pulsing, and the hall was very quiet.

The Throne's judge of the dead, [Nebet](../heroes/nebet.md), set her scale before it. In the pan she laid the feather she had weighed every
king's heart against, which they say is one of Anka's, taken from the sea on the morning after the Fall. In the other pan she laid nothing,
because nobody can lift a sun's heart into a scale; she held the pan up to its light instead, and the light weighed it. It sank.

"Heavier," Nebet said, as the office requires. Then, which the office does not allow, she said why. "With grief. Not with sin."

Sefu had come up the Tower to sit in the Noon Seat and become the new sun, because the Father had come home to his children and the crown of
the Sands was the Father's crown. He looked at the heart of the thing his line had called father for six hundred years, weighed, and wounded,
and heavier with grief than any king, and he understood that it had not come home to anyone. It had been taken, and was trying to get back.
He did not say anything for a whole floor. On the thirty-first he gave the crook to his sister to carry, without a word, and she carried it.

The Throne's veiled priestess of the tombs, [Lithea](../heroes/lithea.md), had come up to look at the Heart with her veil lifted, to see
whether a sun could be turned to stone. She lifted the veil. The Heart did not turn to stone. It looked back at her, the way the sun looks
back at a face turned up to it, and the marble vein in her throat that her own gaze had left there years ago went warm, and she put the veil
down and wept, and would not say why.

## The night the crystals went out

The thirty-first floor was the far halls of the Deep on the night their crystals went out, with the miners standing in the dark where the
light had left them, and a hermit listening to the rock. The **Echo-Hermit** keeps the Deep's ten floors. He has listened to the rock so long
that the rock has started to answer, and the company found him with his ear to a wall, saying that there was something on the other side
digging up.

The gate was held by the Tower's echo of [Opaline](../heroes/opaline.md), with a ring of prisms round her head. The real Opaline was waiting
for them in the dark, and so, to everyone's surprise, was her sister. [Silvane](../heroes/silvane.md) had come up the Deep's floors with a
mirror in her hand, the hand mirror in which she had caught the sun's last light at twelve and never let it out. The sisters had not spoken in
sixty years. They did not speak now. Opaline broke the Deep's light into its colours against her echo, and Silvane turned the colours back with
her mirrors, and between them they made a light the echo had not been kept with, and it stood aside. Then Opaline said, "You've been watching me
through a mirror for sixty years," and Silvane said, "Yes," and that was the end of it, and they walked on together.

[Mabel](../heroes/mabel.md)'s lantern crystal, which still holds the first light of the Shining, sang its high note all the way through the far
halls. [Tova](../heroes/tova.md) closed three galleries on the lurkers coming up out of the deep rock, from the tunnel Mabel had once found and
filled in, a tunnel dug from below: it was one of the Tower's roots, pushing up. [Hesk](../heroes/hesk.md) heard his first golem through the rock,
the one that had walked into the black water with no word on its brow, still walking, and walking up.

The Deep's rulers: the **Prism Mother**, a crystal the size of a hall that turned its beams in the dark to find the others; the **Hollow Queen**,
an Old Mother of the mole-folk who dug too deep alone; the **Lamp Warden**, who swore the lamps would never go out and went out with them; the
**Glass Abbot**, who cut his own eyes into crystal; **Mother of Echoes**, who answers every step a third time; the **Seam King**, who worked his
miners in the dark; the **Gem Tyrant**, buried by the light he hoarded; and the **Drowned Lantern**, a lamp that fell into the black water and
kept burning under it. And one more, on the thirty-seventh floor.

## The Blind Cantor

He sat on a ledge above the black water in the white and gold of the hundred, a big man, a bass, with his eyes burned white, singing the Hymn of
Noon to the dark on the beat, as he had been singing it for a hundred years. He had been taken with the choir in the hour of noon; the Tower had
filed him here, in the dark, because the sun's cracking had blinded him in the instant it took him, and a blind singer belonged, to the Aumbry's
way of keeping, with the night the light went out. The climbers had called him a ruler, because he would not let them pass his ledge while the
Hymn was being sung.

[Cantor](../heroes/cantor.md) knew his voice in the first bar. It was the bass who had sat across from him in the Sun Court for ten years.

Cantor did not fight him. He sat down on the ledge beside him and sang the Hymn with him, on the beat, the two voices together for the first
time since the Fall, and when they came to the end of the Hymn proper, where the last verse begins, the Blind Cantor stopped, as he always
stopped, because only the master knew it.

"Who was behind the beat?" Cantor asked him. "On the day. I have always believed it was Ansel."

The Blind Cantor laughed, a bass laugh, a hundred years old. "Ansel? Ansel was the steadiest tenor in the hundred. It was the girl, at the
master's right hand. A hair behind, in the second line, and you turned your head to her, I saw you. And then the master was gone, before he could
breathe for the verse, and then we all were. Everyone who was on the beat." He turned his white eyes toward Cantor. "She did not drop it, brother.
It took us because we were in step. It missed her because she wasn't. And it missed you because you were looking at her."

Cantor went back down the stair to the company and found Mum, and sat by her, and told her, in front of everyone, what the Blind Cantor had said.
She listened. Then, for the first time in a hundred years, she put her hand on his.

Auriel had heard too. Her father had been the steadiest tenor in the hundred.

## The keeper himself

At the fortieth floor, in a cold white court like the one at the Sea's crown, the Sun Warden was waiting again, and he was not on his knees.

He was himself: Iselm, who had come down from the Sun Court at the top because the word made him guard the Tower's crowns, and because he had
wanted, for a hundred years, to see who would come. He remembered every climber he had met on the tenth floor, though it had not been him there.
He fought them because he had to, and he fought them hard, because he had to know whether they were enough. They were.

When it was over he sat down on the edge of his dial, an old man in gold and white, and [Shiori](../heroes/shiori.md) opened her book.

"I cannot tell you what it is," he said. "The word will not let me tell anyone. It does not forbid me to walk with a guest."

He walked them up one stair further, to a window, as he had walked the first king of the Sands six hundred years before. Through it they saw a
country of terraces round a valley, white stone and blue glass, under a great red sun that did not move, and people on every terrace standing still
and glad in the noon light. At a high window of a pale tower in the middle of it a girl of nine sat up in bed with the sun on her face, as if she
were halfway through a question.

He could not tell them, but they could ask, and he would not lie: when the answer was no, he said no. So they asked, and Shiori wrote down every
question, and he did not say no once. Whether the Tower had been made. Whether he had made it, which took him a long time not to answer. Whether that was his world. Whether the girl at the window
was his. Whether it kept what was ending. Whether, to it, everything was. Whether it had taken their sun. Whether it would take the rest at noon on
midsummer. Whether it would leave, after. Whether the Unmaker was what was left of that world, behind it.

"What is its word?" asked [Hesk](../heroes/hesk.md), the golem-maker, who had come up with the Underforge's party to the Deep's crown and would
go no higher. "Every made thing has one."

The Warden looked at him for a long time, and then smiled, which none of them had seen him do. He could not say the word, because the word would
not let him speak against itself. But he could say his own name, and he did.

"Iselm," he said. "In my tongue it means *keep*."

[Kohaku](../heroes/kohaku.md) heard it the way she hears every spell said aloud near her: as a thing she could take. "Then it's your name," she
said. "Its word is your name. I could steal it."

"Then I would be nothing," said Iselm.

"Would you mind?"

He thought about it, as a careful man does. "No," he said. "Not if something else were written in its place."

Before they went on, Shiori asked him to teach her to write it: not *keep*, which she would not write, but the way his people wrote. He taught
her the script of Senmere on the white floor of the fortieth floor, with her brush, until the slip made it the next day. Hesk watched, and learned
it too, and went down. Mum asked him nothing at all. When the company climbed on, he did not go with them. He went up the other stair, the one only
he used, to the Sun Court, to be at his dial when they came.
