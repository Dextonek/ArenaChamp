# 13. After

*Part five, the first days under the new sun. What came out of the Tower, what went across, and what each of them got.* ·
[The Long Noon](README.md)

## The first afternoon

The new sun did what the old one had done before the Fall, and what nobody under a hundred remembered a sun doing. It walked. It went
down the sky from the Tower in the afternoon, gold and then red, and the Tower's shadow walked round the Dial ahead of it, and at the
hour of dusk, the true hour, the sun set in the Outer Sea in the west and the sky over Gloaming went dark at the right time. Then it was
night, a whole night, with stars, and no Tear among them. In the morning the sun came up out of the sea in the east, over the morning
cliffs of Aubade, and the bells rang it up, and it rose.

The Hourkeepers' Slip-sheet for that month was one line long. *The hours run true.*

## The market comes out

The Tower let go the way an old man opens a box of letters, one at a time, reading each, and the first thing it opened was the first thing
it had taken after the sun.

On the morning after the Hundredth Midsummer the gate of the Tower opened from inside, and out of it, onto the grey stone of the crater, walked
the midsummer market of year 0: a mile of stalls and a thousand families, glass from the Sands and wool from Frostfall and Gloaming wine and
iron from the hill people who were not yet grey, all of them blinking in the sunlight and wondering where the grass had gone. It was still, for
them, a few minutes after noon on midsummer day. Among them, with a paving stone in his arms, came a Kilnborn stonecutter who was not yet grey,
looking for his youngest son, who was meant to be sweeping the wrestling circle.

The Arbiter of the Ring came down out of his box. He was a hundred and fourteen and looked thirty-four, in a black helm with a red crest and a
black and ivory tabard, and his skin had gone grey as ash in his twenties as Kilnborn skin does, even far from the forge. His father did not know
him. Then he did. [Referee Whistler](../heroes/referee-whistler.md) took off his helm in front of forty thousand people, which he had never done,
and his father put down the paving stone and looked at his son's face, a grown man's face, grey, a hundred years late, and said the only thing a
stonecutter could think of to say, which was that the boy's line was still crooked.

Whistler laughed. The Ring had not heard him laugh in a hundred years.

The Countess of Gloaming's steward's daughter walked out of the market with a basket of Coast fish, and was met at the crater's rim by a very old man
from Lantern-under-Hill who had been told, at dawn, by a messenger of the Court, to come. Grandma Ironknit, in the front row with her needles, picked
up two stitches she had dropped a hundred years before, and knitted them back into the cloth, and put a third, which had never ended, back where it
belonged, with a little knot.

[Mr. Ledger](../heroes/mr-ledger.md) walked through the market with his red book, looking at every stall, because a market of the old coinage was an
accountant's dream, and on a money-changer's tray, among Aubade bells and Sands crooks and Gloaming moons, he found a single bronze obol of the Truce,
stamped with the Ring and the Tower's shadow, struck forty years after his death, which a Hundred Door had let fall into the market long ago and which
the money-changer had been puzzling over, in his hour, for a hundred years. It was the coin missing from his sum. He bought it back, at a fair rate.

## The choir comes down

At noon the ninety-eight came down the Tower's stair, singing, as the choir had come down every evening for four hundred years, with the light still
on their faces.

They came down the Dawn Road to Aubade in the afternoon. At the foot of the road, where the families of the hundred had waited until the last light a
hundred years before, there was a crowd waiting again, because the whole city had come out. In it was an old woman of a hundred and twenty-five who
looked forty-five, Noon-touched, with her daughter beside her in white and gold plate. The steadiest tenor in the hundred came down the road and saw his
wife, who had been nine months gone when he left her that morning, and his daughter, who was a hundred years old and looked twenty, and he stopped on the
road with the choir going on round him, and [Auriel](../heroes/auriel.md) ran up it, as she had never run up anything.

The bells rang a hundred, that night, and none of them was for anyone missing.

The master of the choir went to [Mum](../heroes/mum.md) when they reached the city and asked her, as masters do, whether she would teach the last verse
to someone else, now, in case anything happened to her. She said yes. She said it aloud. It was the first word she had spoken in a hundred years, and
[Cantor](../heroes/cantor.md), who was standing next to her because he had decided he would always be standing next to her, said that it was the most
beautiful note he had ever heard, and she told him he was a hair behind, and he was.

## The dead go across

Not everyone the Tower had kept could walk out. Many of the people of its floors had been dead when it took them: the drowned dockers on the quays of
Sallowmouth, the ashen soldiers of the Banners, the old of Gloaming dying in the Long Dusk who could not finish. A keeper that remembers does not keep
the dead from going. They went.

On the Climb's floors the rivers of pale light that the ferryman of the dead lays wherever he poles his boat had run uphill for ten years, because the
dead of the floors could not cross. Now they ran down. [Ostrel](../heroes/ostrel.md) poled his boat up through the Tower floor by floor, and took them
across, all of them: the drowned of the Sea, the dead of the Wood's first year, the buried caravans of the Sands, the miners of the far halls, the ashen
dead of both banners. On the Deep's floors, in the black water, he found a girl's body that had been in the Tower for four hundred years, since the night
he drowned carrying it across a flood, and finished her crossing. It was the crossing his Reason had kept him for. He did not lie down. He found, to his
surprise, that he had become fond of the work.

[Mortis](../heroes/mortis.md) helped him, which nobody had ever seen and Ostrel did not mention. The Restless of the Dial lay down that summer in numbers
the Ossuary Lord had never recorded, as the light grew and the curve on his desk turned over and fell. He sat at his desk one night and wrote the sentence he
had died finishing, three hundred years before: the answer to why the dead get up. *The dead get up because something will not let them end.* He wrote all
of it but the last word, and looked at it a long time, and put the pen down, and went out to see about the War's dead. He is keeping the last word for a
night when he is tired.

[Marin](../heroes/marin.md) danced the tide out of the Drowned Harbour on the first floor, as she had danced it out of the temple, and the drowned of
Sallowmouth went down the stair with her and out to the real harbour and into the sea, home; and a boy with a coral flower behind his ear looked back at her
once. The Bell-Warden stopped ringing. [Gus](../heroes/gus.md) swept the temple floor behind them, and left it wet.

## What came out of the floors

The living came out of the gate for weeks.

From the Sea came the Bell-Warden and the Net-Father and Tam the net-mender, a third of Sallowmouth, to a coast where their grandchildren were old; and the Bell Below, the old bell's ringer, who walked out with [Campana](../heroes/campana.md)'s cracked bell in his arms and gave it to her, and she carried it home to Aubade and hung it beside the new one. From the Wood came the
Moss-Abbess, both of her, the young one walking out arm in arm with the old one, and the Moot speaker of year 90, and the Elder Root: the oak Fenna had planted at twelve walked out of the Tower's gate on its own roots, in the old man's shape the Tower had given it, and stood in the crater, where the Walking Wood had stopped at the edge of Morwen's salt,
and put its roots down, and was a tree again, and the Wood round it stopped walking and stood still, for the first time since the Fall, round the Ring, as a forest. The cut on
[Hank](../heroes/hank.md)'s hand healed in a day. [Fenna](../heroes/fenna.md) planted a flower at the oak's foot.

From the Sands came the caravan towns of the Storm Canyon and a star-reader who climbed the first hill she found and saw, for the first time in her life,
the stars. From the Deep came the miners of the far halls, and the Echo-Hermit, who said the rock had gone quiet. From the War came the living of both
banners: the Last Marshal, who went to the Barrow of Harrow and stood over his king's grave and read him back his own orders; the Herald; the White Sister,
home to Lowlight; the Camp-Mother, who went to work at the Truce Table with Cleaver; Sir Aldous of Nine, on his ninth life, who went back to the Keep of
Nine with his brother to live it out slowly; and a drummer boy of thirteen whom [Madame Cleaver](../heroes/madame-cleaver.md) took home and fed, and kept
feeding, and in time adopted. The Black Herald had delivered his orders, and lay down.

Of the climbers who had gone up the Tower between year 90 and year 99 and never come down, most came down.

## The Noon-touched

The Hourkeepers had feared, for a hundred years, that if the noon were ever let go, everyone held a little in it would age all at once. It was let go.
Nobody aged at all.

What the Tower had held of the Noon-touched was not owed. It had not been kept from them; it had only been kept. When the door opened, nothing came due.
They began, that summer, to age as people always had: one year in one. Auriel will look twenty-one next summer, and twenty-two the summer after, and she has
said it is the best news of her life. Cantor and Mum are growing old together, at the ordinary speed, which they both consider very fast. The Arbiter
will be thirty-five by the Opening.

[Tempo](../heroes/tempo.md) did not become one. The two of him had walked into the Sun Court a heartbeat apart, and a heartbeat apart they stayed: the
Tower was a clock again, and a clock at the Dial's centre needs a keeper at its dial now that its maker has gone. One Tempo stayed at the top, at Iselm's
great dial, winding it. The other went home to Frostfall, to the great clock in the observatory. They keep time a heartbeat apart, and the Dial's hours
run true between them, and neither has ever asked which one is himself.

## The debts

[Referee Whistler](../heroes/referee-whistler.md) gave his ruling on the steps of the Tally-house, on the first evening, under the new sunset: the
Tower had been bound by the fifth clause of the Truce, like any party that sat in the Truce's shadow; it had taken, and it was paying. The Truce held.
The Ring would open next midsummer as it always had. The gate would open, as the fourth clause said, for any champion of the Ring, and it would be the
Truce's to keep.

[Mr. Ledger](../heroes/mr-ledger.md) wrote the Tower's account into the Tally that night, every taking and every return, in the plainest hand he had: the
longest page in the book. With the coin from the money-changer's tray the Tally balanced, for the first time in a hundred years, to the last obol, which
should have finished his Reason and laid him down. It did not, because he has always kept one debt open on purpose, his own, to Grandma Ironknit, for the
scarf. He still wears it under the collar.

The Countess [Vesper](../heroes/vesper.md) paid the principal on the Court of Dusk's note, the grain of the Long Dusk, a hundred years late, in full, in
person, and took no receipt. Her Court lives by night, as Gloaming did before the Fall, and stays indoors at noon, as Gloaming did then too. It has found
a new purpose: Gloaming's Long Dusk is still in the Tower, among the keepings the Tower has not yet opened, and when it opens, somebody must walk its
dying out of it, and the Court of Dusk has said it will.

Asenet paid Vitriol for the curse at last, with one obol, the price never having been set, and the embalmer, who could have lifted it any night for a
hundred years, lifted it that night. The crown of the Sands stayed where Sefu had put it, on the Noon Seat, on no head, and is there still.
[Sefu](../heroes/sefu.md) stopped drinking his draught. He rules the Sands with his sister now, which they both dislike, and the Throne's judge weighs
every hard question for them, and finds most of them heavy with grief.

## The others

[The Great Zambini](../heroes/the-great-zambini.md) has stopped fading. The whole Dial watched him at noon on the Hundredth Midsummer, and the Dial does not
forget a man who made a sky stop falling with a hat; he is solid to the fingertips, and thirty-nine, and insufferable. [Big Yoko](../heroes/big-yoko.md)
walked out of the Ring for the first time in a hundred years and went home to the shrine cliffs below Aubade, and came back for the Opening, and stood in the
circle again, because he liked it. [Brannoc](../heroes/brannoc.md)'s coal burns again, from the Heart's fire freed in the Caldera; he kept the promise he and
Cleaver had made in the Winter of Mud, and the inn at the Waymeet is open. [Professor Puff](../heroes/professor-puff.md) took the basket up the outside of the
Tower at last and mapped every floor's sky, and [Ada](../heroes/ada.md) went down to the room under the Heart to look at the gears and came back saying there
were none, only roots, and a word. Bo's sheep, on the high pastures, at noon on midsummer, did not turn toward the Tower. They went on eating.
[Lyra](../heroes/lyra.md) charted the stars over the closed Tear on the first night, and found them on no chart, and found among them, larger than before, her
red star.

[Anka](../heroes/anka.md) did not come back. She had asked whether an Ember could choose to go home, and been told yes, and she is the sun's warmth now, which
everyone on the Dial can feel on their face at noon. But [Nebet](../heroes/nebet.md) still has her feather, and on the morning of the next midsummer the
fishermen off the Drowned Coast saw a spark fall out of the new sun into the sea, and did not go out. Mortis has been told. He says he will believe it when he
has buried it.
