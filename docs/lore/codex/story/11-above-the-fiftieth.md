# 11. Above the Fiftieth

*Part five, the top. Three realms the Tower never showed, the choir still singing, the noon it took, and a window onto the first world
it kept.* · [The Long Noon](README.md)

## The back rooms

Above the fiftieth floor the stair turned to gold and the floors lost their numbers. No climber had been here. These were the Tower's back
rooms, where a keeper keeps its best pieces, and it had not shown them to anyone in a hundred years.

The first was white. It was Frostfall in **the Long Hold**, the winter after the Fall that did not end until the next summer: the moors
under snow to the horizon, the herds lying down in it, the shore villages of the frozen lake dark under a sky that could not decide what
hour it was. In a croft above the lake a shepherd's family sat round a cold hearth, waiting for spring, as they had been waiting for a
hundred years. Out on the ice, far off, a green and violet aurora hung over the lake at noon. There was no Winter Warden on the ice: the
Tower had not been able to take her, because she had been fighting the cold with everything she had, and it had taken the people she was
fighting for instead.

Here [Tempo](../heroes/tempo.md) found himself.

He had seen himself on the War floors, a heartbeat ahead, going up. He had not understood. Now, on the snow above the lake, a white-haired
man in a long dark coat with a clock-dial halo came toward him out of the falling snow, a heartbeat ahead of him in every step: the half of
him the Tower had kept in the hour of noon, which had been climbing toward the Sun Court for a hundred years because it belonged to that hour
and wanted to go back to it. The two of them stood face to face on the moor. Neither knew which was himself. Then the kept one said, "Again,"
and the other said, "And again," and they laughed, the same laugh a heartbeat apart, and walked on up together.

## Aubade at dusk

The second was gold and grey. It was Aubade at dusk on the day of the Fall: the bell quarter, the Dawn Road running down from the morning
cliffs, and at its foot the families of the hundred, waiting, with every bell in the city ringing because nobody knew what else to do. Far
off up the road a girl with good legs was running toward them with a lamp in her hands, and she would never arrive, because this was the
moment before she did.

[Auriel](../heroes/auriel.md) walked through the crowd. She was a hundred and looked twenty. She had been born at first light the next
morning, and she knew her mother was not here, because her mother's waters had broken at dusk and she had gone home; but everyone else was,
every family of the ninety-eight, holding each other at the foot of the road in their best clothes, looking up at the Tower for the singers
to come down. They had been looking for a hundred years. The hundredth bell, which in her Aubade is always one short for the choir, was
ringing here with all the others, because here nobody yet knew it should be short.

She stood among them and looked up the road with them, the Dawn Paladin of the Noonsworn, in white and gold plate, and for a while she did
not say anything at all. When she went on, she said to Cantor that she understood now what the Noonsworn were: a city that had stood at the
foot of a road for a hundred years, waiting, and had called it faith.

## Gloaming in the Long Dusk

The third was dark. It was Gloaming in **the Long Dusk**, the year without a day: the castle-city round the Night Temple under a sky that
was always just past sunset, the vintners in their cellars and the sick in their beds and the light going out of the heights and never coming
back. It was very beautiful. It was the age the Court of Dusk had called the Dial's true one.

The Countess [Vesper](../heroes/vesper.md) walked through her own city in the year that had made her. She had not been taken in it; she had
spent that year feeding, and fighting the dark with blood, and the Tower takes only those who stand still. The ones who had stood still were
here. She went into a house she knew, in the vintners' street, where a family she had known had nursed their old through the dark, and the old
were in their beds, dying, as they had been dying for a hundred years, and could not finish. She stood by them a long while. Then she came out,
and found [Nyx](../heroes/nyx.md) waiting in her shadow, and said, "This is what we were keeping," and Nyx said nothing, which was the right
thing to say.

These three realms are the Tower's deepest keepings, and the Story passes through them quickly, because each is a chapter for another telling:
Frostfall in the Long Hold, Aubade at dusk, Gloaming in the Long Dusk.

## The Sun Court

The stair came up out of the dark of Gloaming into white light, and the company stepped out onto the Sun Court at the Tower's top, above the
clouds.

It was as it had been on the day of the Fall. It was the day of the Fall. A court of white stone open to the sky, the great dial set into its
floor, the lens at its edge through which the midsummer lamp had been lit; and across it, in rows, in their white and gold, ninety-eight singers
of the choir of a hundred, standing still mid-note, their mouths open on the second line of the Hymn of Noon, in the hour of noon, where they had
been standing for a hundred years. The master stood at their head with his breath drawn for the last verse. A gentle tenor stood one place to the
right of an empty place.

Auriel walked down the rows until she was in front of him. She had her father's face. He was thirty, and did not see her.

Mum walked past them all to the master's right hand, to the empty place where she had stood, and stood in it, and Cantor took his place at the
master's left. For a moment it was the choir of a hundred again, with two of its singers a hundred years older than the rest.

Iselm was at his dial. He had come up his own stair and was waiting, as he had been waiting for every choir for four hundred years, and he
received the company as if they were late. They were not. It was the night before the Hundredth Midsummer.

## The Noon Seat

Above the Sun Court there was one more step: a round dais of white stone, open to the sky, where the sun had rested at noon. Climbers had called
it the Noon Seat, and the Dial had said for a hundred years that it was empty, and that whoever reached it would decide what hung there. From below,
through cloud, it had only ever looked like light.

It was not empty. Over the Seat, so close you could have touched it, hung the noon the Tower had taken.

It was not the sun. The sun's body had cracked and fallen; its heart was in the Hall of Noon a mile below. This was the rest of it, the part the
Aumbry had been able to keep: its light, and its hour, and the gold of it, held still at the instant of noon on midsummer day a hundred years ago.
It hung over the dais like a held breath, a gold so bright that it was quiet, and it did not warm anything, because warmth is a thing that moves,
and nothing in it moved. Shiori wrote that it was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and the saddest, and then crossed out the second
half, because it was not a measurement, and then wrote it in again.

[Anka](../heroes/anka.md) stood under it and burned, a little, without meaning to. She had come up the Tower to find the rest of the fire she
came from. It was here, and it was not fire. It was what was left of the sun when you took away the burning.

## The last window

Above the Seat, where the stair should have ended, a narrow stair went on up, and at its top was a window.

[Pim and Pom](../heroes/pim-and-pom.md) found it first, because nobody stopped them, and went through it, because nobody ever has. On the other
side was the first world the Aumbry ever kept: Senmere, at its noon, a country of terraces under a red sun that did not move, white stone and blue
glass and gardens and cats on the warm walls, and people on every terrace standing still and glad, as they had stood since before the Dial was
counted.

Not everyone was standing still. On a high terrace, under the red sun, children were playing. They had been at noon so long that they had
forgotten every game but one, which they had invented themselves out of the only new thing that had happened in their sky in a thousand years: a
silver thread that came down through it, now and then, from somewhere else, and that you could pull out, stitch by stitch, with small careful
fingers, if you were patient. They had been unpicking [Thessa](../heroes/thessa.md)'s stitches in the Tear for twenty years. They did not know
what the thread was for. They thought somebody was playing with them.

Pim and Pom taught them hide and seek, properly, which is a game you can only play in a place where people move. It took the whole night. The
children of Senmere were very bad at it and loved it. At the end of the night the girl at the high window of the pale tower in the middle of
the city, who had been nine for a thousand years, leaned out and asked the twins what it was like, down there, where things changed. She had sent
the same question down once before, on the tail of a kite that came up through the cloud. Pom told her. Pim said she should come and see.

## The night before

On the Sun Court, among the singers who could not hear them, the company sat with Iselm through the night, and asked him questions, and he did not
say no.

The Tower would close its hand at noon. It would take the Ring and every champion in it, and the last of the Heart's light, and Frostfall and Aubade
and Gloaming whole, not their hours but the realms themselves, and pull up its roots, and go out through the Tear into the dark, with everything it
had kept, to find another ageing sun. It would leave behind a Dial with no sun, no hours and no Ring: the Ring Wick had seen through the hundredth
door, at night, empty.

It could be stopped only by its word. Hesk had been right: a made thing obeys the last word written on it. The Aumbry's word was cut at its root,
in the small room under the floor where the shaft began, beneath the Hall of Noon, and it was Iselm's name, and so it was also in Iselm.
[Kohaku](../heroes/kohaku.md) could take it out of him, as she had taken her brother's name out of his mouth in year 40, so that for one breath the
Tower would have no word at all. In that breath another word could be cut at the root, and the Tower would obey that one.

Which word, they argued half the night. *End*, said the Unmaker, which was an ending and knew what it wanted. *Go*, said Sefu, who had come to
hate the Tower. *Let go*, said Cantor, because that was what the last verse said, and Mum nodded. [Shiori](../heroes/shiori.md), the last of the
Hourkeepers' great calligraphers, whose writing comes true, said none of those. The Hourkeepers have always known what to do with a thing that
cannot be kept: you write it down, and let it go, and keep the record.

"*Remember*," she said.

Iselm did not answer that one either way. After a long time he said, "That would have been a better word," and looked out at the terraces of
Senmere through the window above the Seat, and at the girl at her window, and did not say anything else.

Shiori wrote the word in the script of Senmere, which he had taught her on the fortieth floor, on a single sheet of the Hourkeepers' paper, with
her tall brush, in one stroke, as her way requires. Then they needed someone to carry it a mile straight down, to the room under the Heart, between
the first line of the last verse and the last, and the last verse is not long.

[Pip](../heroes/pip.md) said that a letter is not delivered until it is delivered by hand, and held out his hand.

Toward dawn, up the outside of the Tower, through the cloud, came a paper dragon on a long line, with a young woman of the Spires steering it
from its back and a young man beside her with a very long jade scarf, both of them shouting. [Lian](../heroes/lian.md) had answered the message on
her kite's tail at last. [Zephyr](../heroes/zephyr.md) had come to stand on the Noon Seat for one heartbeat, and then jump. He looked at Pip, and
at the page in Pip's hand, and at the mile of air below the Sun Court's edge, and grinned.

"Race you down," he said.
