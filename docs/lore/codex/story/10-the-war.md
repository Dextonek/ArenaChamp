# 10. The War

*Part four, the Climb, floors 41 to 50. A war that cannot end, a cook, a horn, and the first of the Tower's hours to be let go.* ·
[The Long Noon](README.md)

## The night before the battle

The forty-first floor is the Field of Banners on the last night of the war, in the spring of year 2: the siege line, the burned farm,
the field hospital, the old fort and the ruined keep, signal fires on the ridges, and two armies drawn up in the mud for a battle that
has not been fought. Crimson and azure footmen, bowmen and knights skirmish across it all night, and the ashen soldiers, the dead of both
banners from two years of war, fight beside them. Arrows fall on a line. Nobody inside has told them the war is over, and they would not
believe it, because for them the Ninth Field is tomorrow.

The keeper of the War's floors is **the Herald**, who speaks for both banners and goes from camp to camp with a white staff to arrange a
truce, and has been going for a hundred years; nobody has listened yet. The **Turncloak** keeps the camp, a lynx knight of the Ninefold on
his last life who will not take his helm off. The **White Sister**, born in Lowlight in Aubade, mends the wounded of both sides in the field
hospital. The **Camp-Mother** feeds both armies from one fire on the odd floors, and says she learned the trick from a cook called Cleaver.
The **Quartermaster** sells the stores on the even floors to whoever pays.

The gate was held by the Unmaker. Not the Tower's echo of it this time, though the echo was there: the Unmaker itself had come.

## The Unmaker at the edge of the Field

[Oblivion](../heroes/oblivion.md) had climbed alone, from the gate, floor by floor, a day behind the company, as it had climbed before. It
had never fought its way past the War's gate. The thing that held the gate was itself, the Tower's keeping of it as it was on the night it
walked across the Field, curious, not yet knowing what it was; and it could not unmake itself.

It knew now. The company had left word for it on the white floor of the fortieth, in Shiori's hand, where it would pass. It said what
Iselm had shown them: that the Unmaker was the evening of a world called
Senmere, the ending that world was never allowed to have, dragged behind the Tower from one sky to the next; that it had been pulled through
the Tear at noon by the taking, as it had always believed; and that it wanted the Dial unmade only because it was itself an ending with nothing
to end.

Oblivion read it twice. Then it walked up to its own echo at the Field's edge, the shape of black cracked with pink in the form of an
armoured man on a terrace of Senmere, and said the thing it had been trying to say for a hundred years and had not had the words for.

"There was something here," it said. "There was."

The echo, which had been kept at an hour when it did not yet know, looked at the Unmaker as it was now, and knew. It came apart like smoke,
and the gate of the War stood open, and the Unmaker walked through it, and caught up with the company it had fought for a hundred years, and walked beside it.

## The cook comes back

[Madame Cleaver](../heroes/madame-cleaver.md) had climbed to the forty-first floor once before, found a woman at a cauldron feeding both
armies from one fire, and come down without a word. She had never told anyone why. It was not the Camp-Mother she could not face. It was the
drummer.

On the first night of the Winter of Mud she had caught a starving azure boy stealing from her pot, and fed him. He was the drummer of the
azure, and on the last night of the war the Tower had taken him with the rest, and he was here, on the forty-eighth floor, a boy still inside the shape the Tower had given him, a horned giant in the siege
line's iron, beating the advance on his drum; and when he beat it the ashen dead got up and marched. Climbers called him the **War Drummer** and fought their way past
him. Cleaver had never been able to climb that far.

This time she came up with her cauldron on her back, with the company, and she did not turn back at the forty-first. She found the Camp-Mother
at her fire in the ruined orchard, a woman of her own age, as she had been, with her own trick. The two of them looked at each other for a while.
Then Cleaver hung her cauldron beside the Camp-Mother's, and lit it, and they cooked.

It had never worked in a hundred years, the Camp-Mother said. They came, both banners, and ate, and went back and fought.

"It needs a Truce," said Cleaver. "I didn't have one either, the first time. It took a boy with a slate."

## The horn

The Truce was coming up the stairs behind them, two at a time.

[Pip](../heroes/pip.md) the courier had left the Ring at dawn with the Truce's challenge to the Tower in his pack, sealed in black and ivory, and a
second thing the Arbiter had lent him, which no courier had ever carried: the silver horn on its gold chain, the one sound both armies of the Banners
still stop for. The Tithe-Keeper had written the challenge; the Arbiter had signed it; the Truce Table had witnessed it; and a challenge is not real
until it is delivered by hand. Pip had run the Sea's ten floors before breakfast and the Wood's before noon. He came over the ridge of the forty-first
floor at dusk with his gold-winged heels smoking, and found the company at Cleaver's fire, and asked who was in charge, and was told nobody.

He blew the horn.

On every floor of the War, at once, the fighting stopped. The crimson lowered their spears. The azure lowered theirs. The ashen dead stood still in
the mud, the way they had stood still in the real Field when the Arbiter first blew that horn at the Truce's swearing, in year 2, which none of them
had lived to see. In the silence the Herald walked out between the lines with his white staff, as he had walked out ten thousand times, and for the
first time somebody listened.

## The truce on the Field

It took all night. The Field's rulers came to it one by one, because the worst of an hour does not give up easily.

The **Last Marshal** of the forty-first floor, who had refused his king and led the azure and been taken from his tent before the battle, rode
out on his horse of smoke and guarded both banners against the truce, because he had sworn to guard them and the war was all he had left to guard.
[Sir Whiskers](../heroes/sir-whiskers.md) went to meet him. They duelled in the mud by the light of the signal fires, and the Marshal was the
better rider, and the lynx was the better liar, and in the end the Marshal's sword went through the lynx's guard, and Sir Whiskers died, and
landed on his feet, and got up, one life fewer, and said, "Do try again." The Marshal looked at him for a long time and then laughed
and sheathed the sword.

The **Iron Marshal**, the crimson's commander who had held the siege line through the Winter of Mud, came next, and then the **Siege Lord**, an
Underforge engineer who had built the crimson's engines for pay and stayed to see them fire, and the **Ashen General** of the dead. The **Banner Knight**,
the crimson's standard-bearer, had sworn the banner would never touch the ground, and had held it up for a hundred years; Cleaver gave him a bowl of
truce-pot, which needs two hands, and he set the banner down to take it. The **Crow King**, a lord of Gloaming who had ridden to the crimson for a share
of the sun, would not eat until the Countess [Vesper](../heroes/vesper.md) sat down beside him and ate first. The **Black Herald** had been carrying
King Orlan's last orders for a hundred years and had never been allowed to deliver them; Pip delivered them for him, by hand, to the Last Marshal, and
the Marshal broke the seal and read them aloud. They were two words long. King Orlan, the night before the Ninth Field, had written to his enemy: *Come
home.* The **Grey Captain**, who had deserted the azure on that night and regretted it on every floor, sat down at the fire and was not, any more, the
only one who had walked away.

And on the forty-eighth floor Cleaver found the drummer.

He stood a head over the dead, horned and black with iron, beating the advance, with the dead getting up round him as he beat it. She walked up
through them with a bowl in her hand, and he stopped drumming, because he knew her, and took off the horned helm, and under it was a boy of
thirteen, thin. She had caught him by the ear once. She sat him down on his drum and gave him the bowl, and he ate,
and the dead lay down.

## The first hour let go

Toward dawn, at Cleaver's fire, the Herald's truce was made. Both banners ate from one pot. Nobody fought within sight of it. The Last Marshal took
off his helm and said his name, which the azure had not used in a hundred years, and his guard knelt to him in the mud. Sir Aldous of Nine, the
Turncloak, who had fought for both banners one after the other and spent eight lives doing it, took off his helm too, at last, and his younger brother
saw his face for the first time since the Field.

Then something happened that had not happened since the Aumbry was made.

The hour ended. The Ninth Field would never be fought, and the night before it, which the Tower had been keeping for a hundred years, came to a
morning. The light changed on the Field. The signal fires burned down. The ashen soldiers, the dead of both banners, lay down in the mud and did not get
up, and the living stood over them, and wept, and were tired, as people are at the end of a war. The whole floor shook, from the mud to the sky, the way
a cupboard shakes when something inside it falls.

Far below, in the Ring, the Arbiter felt it in his feet and looked up. Far above, in the Sun Court, Iselm put both hands on his dial.

The Unmaker stood at the Field's edge and watched the war end. It was the one thing on the Dial it had truly unmade, the only war that could not end,
because the Unmaker had walked across it; and it had ended anyway, because a cook and a courier and a Field's worth of tired people had decided that
it would. Oblivion had wanted, for a hundred years, to know what losing was. It thought it knew now. It did not mind as much as it had expected.

"So that is how," it said, to nobody, and went on up.

## The sparks again

The War's last floors were quiet after that. The armies were going home, wherever home was for them, now that the Tower could no longer keep them
from it: down the stair, a long line of crimson and azure with the Herald at its head, looking for a gate. The company went on up through them.

At the fiftieth floor, the War's crown and the top of the Climb, in a crater on the Field where the earth was still hot, burned **Ash and Ember**
again: the Tower's keeping of the sun's two last sparks, at the instant they fell, as the Wood's crown had been. Above them, where the crater's sky
should have been, there was a stair, going up into gold.

They fought. They had to. And when they were down, they looked at [Anka](../heroes/anka.md) a third time, and she did not ask.

The brute of basalt on his knuckles spoke for the first time. His voice was a fire settling.

"The hand is opening," he said. "We felt the war end. Come up when it opens all the way. Bring the rest."

Anka asked him what the rest was.

"Everything that fell," said the flame in the burnt mantle. "And the one who carried the lamp."

Above them, up the stair, a great dial struck the hours of a day a hundred years gone, and it was nine weeks, near enough, since the company had come
through the gate, and three days to midsummer.
