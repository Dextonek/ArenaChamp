# 3. Midsummer

*Part two, the Fall. One day, a hundred years ago, from first light to the next morning, as the Dial lived it and as it was.* ·
[The Long Noon](README.md)

## First light

At first light on midsummer day the bells of Aubade rang the sun up, as they had every morning for a thousand years, and it came up
gold over the morning cliffs, a little paler than anyone's grandmother remembered, and nobody minded. It was the longest day of the
year. Everyone was out.

At the Tower's foot the midsummer market was already loud. It was the biggest gathering on the Dial: a mile of stalls on the green plain
round the Tower, glass from the Sands, wool from Frostfall, Gloaming wine, Coast fish packed in salt, iron from the hill people who were
not yet grey, and a thousand families who had walked for days to eat badly and dance all night. A Kilnborn stonecutter had a contract to
lay new paving at the market's heart, and he had brought his youngest son to sweep for him: a small, argumentative boy of fourteen who
could not cut a straight line but could tell you exactly where everyone else's was crooked. His father sent him off to sweep the
wrestling circle at the market's edge, to get some peace. The boy would be [Referee Whistler](../heroes/referee-whistler.md).

The circle was a ring of straw rope on the grass a mile from the Tower, where shrine wrestlers from the cliffs below Aubade threw each
other for the crowd. The biggest of them that year was a boy of eighteen called Yoko, already a giant, who was warming up by pushing a
cart full of stones round the rope. He threw salt into the circle to keep it clean. Whistler swept it up. They did not like each other
for the first hour.

## The climb

In the morning the choir of a hundred climbed the Tower, as it did every day. At midsummer they carried a lamp, an old lantern-shrine
of brass and glass, to be lit from the sun's own light before noon and sent home to Aubade, where it would hang in the shrine of the
bell quarter for the year: the custom of the midsummer lamp.

Cantor was thirty and the best baritone in the hundred. Mum was twenty-six and sang at the master's right hand, with Cantor at his left
and a gentle tenor called Ansel at her other side. Ansel's wife was nine months gone with their first child in Aubade, and he had told
the whole choir about it twice on the stair. The master was old and climbed slowly. He had not slept. Halfway up he stopped on a landing,
for breath, and took Mum's hand, and said quietly that whatever happened at noon she was to sing the last verse, with him or without
him. She said of course. She did not understand what he meant.

At the top the Sun Court was white and bright, open to the sky, the great dial in its floor striking the hours. The Warden stood at the
dial in gold and white. He received the choir as if they were late, as he always did. The midsummer lamp was lit from the sun through the
great lens at the court's edge, early, as the custom was, and given to a runner, a girl from the bell quarter with good legs, who went
down the stair with it two steps at a time. She would reach Aubade at dusk. The lamp would be called, after that day, **the Ember of
Aubade**, because it was the last light of the old sun that anyone could hold.

## Noon

At noon the choir sang.

The Hymn of Noon is long, and its beat is everything: it was a beat before it was a song. Cantor sang on it, as he always did. In the
second line Mum felt the beat slip under her. She had been thinking about the last verse, about the master's hand on the landing, and
for a hair's breadth of a moment she was behind. She tried to catch up.

Above the Tower the sun stopped.

The Hourkeepers' lens, three hundred miles away, recorded that it stopped for the space of nine heartbeats. To the choir it was longer.
The light did not move. The shadows on the white floor did not move. The Warden at his dial went down on one knee, as if something had
struck him from inside, and put both hands flat on the stone.

The Aumbry had closed its hand.

Everything in the hour of noon at the Tower's top was taken into it. The choir went still, mid-note, ninety-eight singers in their white
and gold, the master first, before he could draw the breath for the last verse; and then they were not there. Cantor saw it happen. He
had turned his head to look at Mum, because she had stumbled on the beat and he loved her, and in that turn he was out of step with the
hour too, and it passed him by. He saw the singers go still, like a painting of a choir, and then the court was white and empty, and
there was only the Warden on his knees, and Mum, and himself.

Ninety-eight voices stopped at once. Mum heard the silence and believed, then and for a hundred years, that it was her fault: that she had
been the hair behind the beat and the Hymn had broken on her. Both halves of that were true, and neither meant what she thought. The beat
she missed had not dropped the sun. It had saved her.

The Tower took the sun's noon: its light, its hour, the gold of it. It could not take its body. The sun was a living fire far larger than
the Aumbry's hand, and for nine heartbeats it was held, and then it cracked, with a sound that was heard from Frostfall to the Coast, and
fell.

## The fall

The heart of the sun came down at the Tower's foot and went into the ground, white, and the earth opened round it into a hall of black
glass, the **Hall of Noon**, where it burns still. Two embers fell into a pit beside it, the **Cinder Pit**, and burn there, and watch each
other: **Ash and Ember**. The rest scattered over the Dial as shards and warm stones and sparks.

The midsummer market was gone. Not burned: gone. The Tower had taken it with the noon, a mile of stalls and a thousand families and a
Kilnborn stonecutter laying paving at its heart, into the hour, where it is still noon on midsummer day and the market is loud. The plain
where it had stood was a shallow crater of grey stone, four miles across, and nothing has grown on it since but a pale moss. A mile away,
in the straw-rope circle, Yoko was thrown off his feet, and a boy with a broom landed on top of him.

The Tower's shadow stopped. Across the Dial it lay where it had lain at noon, a long dark finger across the plain, and it did not move. It
lay across the straw-rope circle, and Yoko, getting up, put his feet in it without knowing, and stood there, and did not know why he did
not want to step out. He did not step out for a hundred years.

Where the sun had hung, the sky tore. The Aumbry had come into the Dial through a hole in its sky, long ago, and the sky had healed over;
now the taking had torn it open again. **The Tear** was black, with a burning pink edge, and through it, for the first time, the Dial saw the
dark between the stars. Something was pressing against the far side. It had been pressing for a thousand years. The Tower had pulled the
sun out of the sky like a nail out of a door, and through the hole, pulled after it, came the evening of Senmere. It stepped out onto the
Tower as a shape of black cracked with pink, very tall, in the form of the first thing it saw, which was an armoured man on a terrace of
Senmere a thousand years and a world away. The Hourkeepers would call it **the Unmaker**. It would call itself
[Oblivion](../heroes/oblivion.md), when it had learned enough of the Dial's tongue to choose a word, and it would spend a hundred years
looking for whatever had pulled it through.

## Across the Dial

Everywhere, at once:

- **On the Coast**, the sun's heat struck the sea off Sallowmouth, the great port of the Drowned Coast, and lifted it over the town in an
  afternoon. In the sea temple a girl of twelve, a dancer, was dancing the noon dance when the water came in, and she did not stop; she
  danced the water back out of the temple with ribbons of tide while half the city drowned round it. She was
  [Marin](../heroes/marin.md). A boy who had given her a coral flower that morning was on the quays.
- **In the Deep**, a young mole-folk miner of twenty, [Mabel](../heroes/mabel.md), saw a light run through the rock like water, and
  into the crystals of the halls, which have glowed ever since: the Shining. In the far halls, past the last forge, the crystals took the
  light and held it for a night, and then went out.
- **Under the southern hills** the cold crater filled with fire from below, and became the Ember Caldera. A master smith of thirty called
  [Brannoc](../heroes/brannoc.md) walked to its edge that night and said it was the best fire he had ever seen.
- **On the Glass Flats** the heat struck the sand and turned it to glass, and the Sands said the Father had come home into their dunes.
  The young queen, [Asenet](../heroes/asenet.md), stood on the palace roof in Tamerit and watched the sky tear, and knew she had been
  right, and knew nobody would ever believe she had not caused it.
- **In the west** the Still Wood pulled up its roots and began to walk east, toward the Tower, as if to find the sun. The Tower's roots
  ran under it, and the Tower had begun to draw. A girl of fifteen named [Fenna](../heroes/fenna.md) watched the oak she had planted at
  twelve walk away with the rest.
- **On the high pastures** above the Wood, every sheep in [Bo](../heroes/bo.md)'s flock turned to face the Tower and stood still for nine
  heartbeats.
- **Over the Spires**, [Professor Puff](../heroes/professor-puff.md), forty and mapping the sky from his basket, felt the wind change
  direction nine times in nine heartbeats, from nine places, and blow toward the Tower each time, as if the Tower were breathing in.
- **In Gloaming**, the light left first. The Countess of the heights, [Vesper](../heroes/vesper.md), thirty-five, stood among her vines
  and watched it go. In the mirror quarter a girl of twelve, [Silvane](../heroes/silvane.md), caught the sun's last light in a hand
  mirror, and closed it, and has never let it out; the light was looking at her when she caught it. On the wall of the Night Temple a
  glazier's shadow, thrown long by that last light, did not move when the light changed. The light that had thrown it had been taken in
  mid-throw, and the shadow belonged to nothing now. It stepped off the wall. It would be called [Nyx](../heroes/nyx.md).
- **In Frostfall**, on the terraces above the frozen lake, a young dancer of the lake-born, [Saga](../heroes/saga.md), tried to do
  what the oldest dancers' song said could be done, and called to the sun at noon to come back. The aurora came instead, green and violet
  across a noon sky. In the observatory the keeper of the great clock, a careful clockmaker of forty, had stopped to look up through the dome
  at the strange light, and was for once a heartbeat behind his own clock; he felt the hour close on him and slip: half of him was taken
  into the noon, and half of him was not. He has lived every moment
  twice since. He is [Tempo](../heroes/tempo.md). The great clock's hand stopped nine heartbeats past noon, and he scratched the place
  into the brass.
- **At the Wood's edge**, in a cottage on the Frostfall road, a Briarkin of the Spindle court who spun the threads of lives, whom the Dial
  would call [Grandma Ironknit](../heroes/grandma-ironknit.md), dropped three stitches at once. One was the choir's master. One was the
  Countess of Gloaming's steward's daughter, who had gone to the market. The third she could not place, because it had not ended: it had
  come loose from the cloth and was still alive, somewhere, unknitted. It was Mum, a hair out of step with every hour from that day on.

## Dusk

At dusk the runner reached Aubade with the midsummer lamp, and the city came out to meet her. The bell quarter had been ringing since noon,
not knowing what else to do. The lamp was the last light of the old sun anyone could hold, and they hung it in the shrine, and the city
stood round it all night.

Two singers came down the Tower's stair in the dark. The Warden had stood up at last and walked them to the stair's head and said nothing,
because there was nothing the word would let him say. Cantor held Mum's hand all the way down. She did not speak. She did not make a
sound, not a word, not a note, not a cry, and she has not made one since.

Ansel's wife waited at the bottom of the Dawn Road with the other families of the hundred until the last light, and then went home,
because her waters had broken.

## The next morning

The next morning the sun did not rise.

What rose instead was a thin light from the Tower's top. It came up late and pale from the Heart in the Hall of Noon, through the Tower, as
heat comes up a flue, and spread over the Dial, and the realms lived by it. Each morning since, it has come up a little thinner, because
the Tower drinks it as it passes.

In Aubade, at first light, Ansel's daughter was born. The Noonsworn, when there were Noonsworn, would take her for a sign, and raise her in
white and gold, and call her [Auriel](../heroes/auriel.md).

Off the Drowned Coast a spark of the sun, which had slipped the Tower's grip and fallen into the sea, walked out of the surf as a woman of
flame and gold, in the shape of the first thing it saw, which was a little girl called Ama dancing on the shore. She was
[Anka](../heroes/anka.md), the first of the Embers.

And on the crater's floor, where the market had been, a fox girl in a mask sat up in the grey dust and looked about her for the revel. The
Tower's broken grip had shaken loose some of its oldest keepings, as a dropped cupboard spills its shelves, and she had fallen out. She had
been at a midsummer dance three hundred years before, and it was still, to her, the same morning. The Fox court did not know her. She took
a new name, as Briarkin do, and the Dial would know her as [Kohaku](../heroes/kohaku.md). Her brother was still inside.
