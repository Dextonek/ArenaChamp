# 4. The Banners

*Part two, years 0 to 2. The realms go to war over the sun's pieces, the Tower takes its first realms, and the Truce is sworn in its
shadow.* · [The Long Noon](README.md)

## The pieces

When the sun broke it fell in pieces, and pieces of a sun are worth a great deal. Shards of its crust lay warm in the fields of the
middle Dial. Glass from the Flats burned for a month if you set it in a hearth. Embers smouldered in hedgerows and did not go out. The
first winter after the Fall was the hungriest the Dial had known, because the harvest had failed under the thin light, and whoever held
a piece of the sun had heat, and light, and a reason for others to obey them.

King Orlan of **Harrowmark**, whose kingdom lay nearest the Tower, said the pieces were his by right of neighbourhood. In the autumn of
year 0 he raised the crimson banner and sent his knights to collect them. The Dune Throne, whose queen had joined him for a share, and some
of the Gloaming houses, rode with him. In the spring of year 1 the other realms answered as **the Azure League**: Aubade's young paladins,
the Coast's levies, the axes of the hill people, the Hourkeepers' surveyors, the hunters of the Wood, and Ninefold knights on both sides,
as there always were. Its commander was a knight of Harrowmark who had refused his king, and whose name the azure did not use, because he
asked them not to. They called him the Marshal. The Dial calls him **the Last Marshal**.

Nobody on either side knew that the Tower was collecting too.

## The first realms

It had the sun's noon, and the choir, and the market. It wanted more, and it knew where to look, because its shadow had learned every
room of the Dial for a thousand years. It took each realm at the hour of its greatest loss, because a world grieves most for what it
loses, and the Aumbry was made to keep what is grieved.

It could not take everyone in an hour. Whoever was fighting the loss with everything they had, a girl dancing the water out of a temple,
a warden holding a frozen lake still, was out of step with the hour, as Mum had been with the noon, and the Tower's hand passed over them.
It took the ones who stood still in their grief, and the places they stood in.

It took the Drowned Harbour first: Sallowmouth on the morning after the Fall, at dawn, with the sea already in its streets and still
rising, the drowned on the quays and the living on the roofs. The town's harbour bell had been ringing all night to call the boats home,
and the woman ringing it, a keeper of the sea temple's tower, was still ringing it when the hour closed on her. She is ringing it now.
Outside, on the real Drowned Coast, the survivors counted their dead and could not find a third of them, and said the sea had taken
them. The sea had not.

It took the far halls of the Deep on the night their crystals went out, with the miners in them standing in the sudden dark, and a hermit
who had come to listen to the rock; the halls outside were found empty, the lamps still warm.

It took a year of the Walking Wood: not an hour this time but a whole first year of walking, because the Wood's loss was slow, and the
Tower had drawn on its roots to make it walk. Inside, the Wood is walking still, in its first year, and the abbess of a moss chapel at its
edge keeps the paths, and a girl's oak, grown into the heart of the forest, drinks from the Tower's own root.

In the spring of year 1 it took the Storm Canyon in the Sands, on the night of the great storm.

## The night of the storm

That night, in Tamerit, the Throne's guard came to Asenet's brother at midnight and offered him the crown. The river towns had risen; the
court had turned; the Sands needed someone to blame for the Father's fall, and they had a queen who had opened the first king's tomb a
year before and come back saying strange things. [Sefu](../heroes/sefu.md) was twenty-four, handsome, a horseman, a speech-maker, and
afraid. He hesitated. He said yes, because he was afraid of what they would do if he said no, and for a hundred years he has told the
Sands that he took it.

[Asenet](../heroes/asenet.md) was driven out that night. She went first to the embalmers' house, to the master embalmer,
[Vitriol](../heroes/vitriol.md), and asked him for a working to lay on the crown: that it would never stay on one head. He brewed it;
she never paid him. Then she went to the gate of Tamerit with the court's guard behind her, stood in the gate's shadow, and laid the curse
on the crown she no longer wore. And she looked at the young lion who captained the gate, [Ramet](../heroes/ramet.md) of the Ninefold,
and said his grandmother's name, which only she at court knew, and he opened the gate and let her go.

The storm came off the Flats an hour later, the worst in the Sands' memory. It buried the caravan towns of the Storm Canyon to their
roofs. The Tower took that night: the canyon, the storm, the raiders waiting for a caravan that would never come, a star-reader counting
the lightning from a beacon tower. Outside, the caravan towns were dug out in the spring and found empty.

Asenet rode north through that storm with sand in her mouth and the curse's twin, a thin circlet that would not stay on any head, in her
hand. She knew what had taken the Father. She had read it on a tomb wall. She knew nobody would believe her, now least of all, and she
was right, for ninety-nine years.

## The Winter of Mud

In the winter of year 1 the war on the Field of Banners stopped moving. Rain fell for three months. Neither banner could advance a mile.
The Field, which had been farmland and orchards round the castle of Harrow, became a sea of mud with two armies drowning in it.

In the ruined orchard of Harrow, between the lines, a caravan cook's daughter of the Sands, twenty-five, built a fire under a cauldron and
fed whoever came. On the first night a starving azure boy, a drummer, stole from her pot; she caught him by the ear and fed him. The next
night crimson soldiers came. She fed them too. By midwinter both armies were eating from one fire under a flag of white linen she had hung
from the cauldron's arm, and nobody fought within sight of it. Her cleaver came from a young smith of the hill people who had walked to the
Field to sell axes and stayed to sharpen her knives, and the two of them made a promise one cold night to open an inn together at the
Waymeet when the war was really over. She was [Madame Cleaver](../heroes/madame-cleaver.md). The smith was Brannoc. Neither kept the
promise.

That winter, too, two Briarkin children, one life between them, were left in a woven satchel on the doorstep of a cottage on the Frostfall
road, and Grandma Ironknit took them in. They were [Pim and Pom](../heroes/pim-and-pom.md). Since the Fall the Briarkin's children were
sometimes born two for one, as if the Tower's grip on the hour of noon had caught each of those lives in a closing door and split it. Their
mother had not wanted to leave them. Her court had made her.

## The night before the Ninth Field

By the spring of year 2 both banners had one battle left in them. They drew up on the ninth field of Harrow, the last unflooded ground,
for the fight that would end it.

The night before, something walked across the Field.

The Unmaker had come out of the Tear onto the Tower, and down it, and had wandered the Dial for two years learning what it was. It did
not know yet. It knew that where it went, things did not end. A candle it passed did not burn down. A wounded man it stood over did not
die, and did not heal. It was the evening of Senmere, the ending that world was never allowed to have, and everything it touched caught
its condition: it could not finish. That night it walked between the two camps, slowly, curious, and where it passed the fires did not
burn low, and the sentries did not grow tired, and the war, which had been about to end, could not.

The Tower felt it the way a keeper feels a draught. A war that could not end was a thing that would never stop being lost, and the
Aumbry was made to keep what is lost. Before dawn it took the hour.

It took the Field as it was on the last night of the war, with both armies on it and the battle not yet fought: the Marshal at the azure's
head, the crimson's standard-bearer and its herald, the cook's fire and the woman who had learned to tend it from Cleaver and was tending it
that night while Cleaver slept in the cart, a drummer boy of the azure with his drum, a field hospital where a sister of Aubade mended both
sides, and a lynx knight of the Ninefold, Sir Aldous of Nine, who had fought for the crimson and then the azure and was on his ninth life.
He was the elder brother of a younger lynx who would one day call himself [Sir Whiskers](../heroes/sir-whiskers.md).

In the morning the Battle of the Ninth Field was fought anyway, by whoever was left. The azure fought without its Marshal and did not know
why he had not come out of his tent. Both banners were broken. King Orlan died in the mud and was buried where he fell, under the Barrow
of Harrow. The Keep of Nine lost sixty knights in a day. The Last Marshal was never found, nor his guard, nor the drummer. The Field became
the Field of Banners, a place of mud and white stones. Inside the Tower, on that night, the Ninth Field has still not been fought, and
the Unmaker stands at the Field's edge like a gate nobody can pass, because it is the reason the war cannot end.

## The circle

Through the two years of the war there was one place near the Tower where nobody fought.

The straw-rope circle a mile from the Tower's foot, where the market's wrestlers had thrown each other, had survived the Fall, and so had
the boy who stood in it. Yoko had not stepped out. He could not have said why. He slept in it, and ate in it, from a pot a cook from the
Field sent over when she heard, and when soldiers of either banner came near he asked them, politely, to choose: the ring was small, and
the world outside it was large. Champions began to come to it instead of to the Field: a crimson knight and an azure knight who agreed to
settle a village's fate one against one, a pair of captains, a Ninefold who wanted the practice. The boy who had been sweeping the circle
when the sun fell swept the sand between bouts, and watched, and wrote down on a slate, in his father's masons' marks, exactly how each of
them cheated.

The circle lay in the Tower's stopped shadow. Nobody noticed that either. It is the one place on the Dial where the Tower's hand lies on
the ground, and a giant boy was standing on it, and while he stood there, the hand could not move.

## The Truce

At midsummer of year 2, when both banners were broken and the Field was mud and the king was buried, the realms came to the circle to
make peace, because it was the only ground near the Tower that nobody had bled on.

They argued for nine days. Every realm wanted its own judge for the duels the peace would need. On the ninth day a Coast captain asked
the boy with the slate, mostly as a joke, what he thought. The boy read them his list. Every founder in the circle was on it. They laughed;
then they stopped laughing, and made him Arbiter, because he was the only person at the Tower's foot whom no realm owned. Madame Cleaver's
cauldron became the first Truce Table, and everyone who swore ate from it first. A dead clerk from Harrow in a black coat, who had been
keeping the accounts of the crimson's supply train because somebody had to, offered to write the first page of the Tally, and nobody
dared refuse him.

They swore **the Truce of the Ring** standing in the circle, in the Tower's shadow, which is to say before the whole Dial: no realm makes
war over the sun again; every quarrel is settled by champions, one against one, in the Ring at the Tower's foot; whoever eats at the Truce
Table may not strike in anger outside the Ring; the Tower's gate opens only for a champion of the Ring; every duel is written in the
Tally, and every debt it settles is paid.

The fourth clause was a flourish. The Tower's gate had been shut since the Fall. The stair the choir had climbed for four hundred years
was behind a door of grey stone with no handle, and nobody could open it. The founders put the clause in so that no realm would try.

The Aumbry keeps what is sworn in its shadow. That is what it is for. That night, on the gate's grey face, where there had been no mark
in a thousand years, a line of letters appeared, cut clean as if by a mason, in the Ring-speech every founder had sworn in:

*The gate opens only for a champion of the Ring.*

The Arbiter saw it in the morning, on his way to sweep, and told nobody for a week, because he did not know which rule it was a breach of.
