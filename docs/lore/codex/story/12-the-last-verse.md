# 12. The Last Verse

*Part five, noon on the Hundredth Midsummer. At the top, at the foot and under the Heart, at once.* · [The Long Noon](README.md)

## Morning at the Ring

At dawn on the Hundredth Midsummer the Ring was full. The Truce had asked the Ringside to go home. Forty thousand people had come anyway,
because it was the Opening of the Ring and the hundredth anniversary of the Fall, and because, if the Tower was going to take the Ring
at noon, they had decided they would rather be in it.

Its officers held it.

[Morwen](../heroes/morwen.md) the Saltwarden walked the Ring's edge before first light with her pouch, as she did every dawn, and then
kept walking: out of the stands, across the crater's floor, round the Ring and the stands and the Truce Table and the Tally-house and the
couriers' loft, a line of salt four miles round. She had always wanted to lay a line round the Tower to see which side it was on. The line
went round the Ring, and the Tower stood outside it.

[Kessa](../heroes/kessa.md) the Chainwarden brought up every chain of the Gaol of Oaths, forged in the Ember Caldera, her grandmother's
chains, and ran them from the Gaol's great anchor under the harbour road all the way to the Tower's foot, and round it, three times, and
locked them. "You can run to the end of the chain," she told it. "Then you come back."

Under the stands, [Wick](../heroes/wick.md) walked the gallery of the Hundred Doors with his lamp. He was a hundred and fifty years old. All
night the doors had been warm, and since dawn they had been pushing, all of them at once, from the other side, like doors in a house where
someone is opening every window. He had sworn to keep them. He put his back against the hundredth, and held it, and the lamp he had kept lit
in front of it for ninety-six years burned steady.

[Referee Whistler](../heroes/referee-whistler.md) stood in the Arbiter's box with no horn, because a courier had it, and a sigil tablet in his
hand cut with the fifth clause. [Mr. Ledger](../heroes/mr-ledger.md) stood beside him with the Truce's bill. [Madame Cleaver](../heroes/madame-cleaver.md),
come down from the War floors with her cauldron, fed the stands. [Grandma Ironknit](../heroes/grandma-ironknit.md) sat in the front row with her
needles and her grey cat, knitting, and her face had the look of a woman who has found a dropped stitch at last.

And in the straw-rope circle at the Ring's heart, where the Tower's shadow had lain since the Fall, [Big Yoko](../heroes/big-yoko.md) stood with
his feet in it, as he had stood for a hundred years, and threw a handful of salt, and waited.

## Noonfall

A mile beneath them, in the hall between the court and the pit, two champions of the Ring went down to fight the Raid's three crowns with the
Truce's leave: [Orren](../heroes/orren.md) the Sunbearer, with the Ember of Aubade in its brass shrine on his back, and
[Itzel](../heroes/itzel.md), the Obsidian Herald of the Underforge, with her blade of black glass and a word in her mouth she had never said.

The Sun Warden's sentinel, the stone and gold likeness the Tower had made of its keeper to guard the court, met them at the first gate. They
broke it. Ash and Ember came up out of the Cinder Pit at the second, two shapes of fire, the sun's last sparks themselves, bound to guard the
Heart. They fought, because they were bound; and when they were down they did not go out. They looked at the brass shrine on Orren's back, and at
the light inside it, and stood aside.

"The one who carried the lamp," said Ember.

Under the hall, in the Hall of Noon, the Heart was waiting. For a hundred years the Tower had drunk its light every morning as it rose; it was a
wounded and furious thing, white and huge, and it had fought every pair of champions the Truce had sent down, and it fought these two. It was
very nearly noon. Above them the whole Tower had begun to hum.

Itzel did what the Caldera's fire had asked her to do when she was fifteen. She walked into the Heart's light, close enough to blister, and said
the word it had given her, aloud, for the first time. It was the sun's own name, older than the Dial, older than the Hymn: the name the sun had
before anyone called it father or noon. The Heart heard its name and was, for a moment, not a wound but a sun, and stopped.

Orren walked into the stillness. He had sworn never to set the Ember down except to fight. The Noonsworn had always said it went up, to the Noon
Seat, to be hung in the sky; the Caldera's fire had always said, to Itzel, that it was waiting for him down here. He unbuckled the brass shrine
from his back, the first time it had been off his back in ten years, and opened it, and set the Ember of Aubade, the last light of the old sun
anyone could hold, into the Heart, as you set a seed in the ground. Then he sat down on the black glass floor beside it, because it was very heavy,
and he was very tired.

Beneath the Heart, the rock that no drill had broken broke.

## The root

The Underforge had been digging toward that rock for nine weeks, under the Heart, in the dark, with the Truce's leave and the Hall of Marks
pretending not to know. [Tova](../heroes/tova.md), the sapper, had at last finished the charge she had never finished on purpose: the one that does not
need to know where the wall is. [Mabel](../heroes/mabel.md) had dug the gallery to it with her claws, following the high note in her lantern crystal.
[Bram](../heroes/bram.md) had come because he had always wanted to touch the Tower's stone and find out who had cut it, and he had brought Gerald.
[Ada](../heroes/ada.md) had built the lift. [Hesk](../heroes/hesk.md) waited at the face with a chisel and a mallet and the script of Senmere in his
head, and Brannoc behind him with a light, because the coal in his chest was the only fire down there.

Tova lit the fuse. The charge burned fuchsia and found the wall, wherever it was, and the rock under the Heart opened like a book.

Behind it was a small room of grey stone with no seam in it, where the shaft began. Roots as thick as towers went out from it in every direction,
under everything. On its wall, cut deep, in a script nobody on the Dial but two people could read, was one word.

Bram put his hand on it. Gerald, under his other arm, said nothing, because Gerald never says anything; but Bram heard, as he sometimes hears stone,
the mason who had cut it, a careful man, grieving. "Oh," said Bram. "Oh, it was him."

Hesk raised his chisel and waited. He could not cut a new word over the old one. A golem obeys the last word written on it, but only while no other
word is there; and this one was the strongest word he had ever seen.

## Noon

At the top of the Tower, at noon, the Aumbry closed its hand.

It did it the way it had done it a hundred years before, the way it had done it on Senmere: all at once, and gently, as a hand closes on a moth. The
Sun Court shook. The ninety-eight singers in their rows trembled like candle flames. Over the Noon Seat the kept noon blazed. Above it the Tear opened,
and opened, a black mouth with a burning pink edge that filled half the sky, and the dark between the stars looked down through it at the Dial. Across
the Dial the ground heaved as the roots began to pull: the Walking Wood broke into a run and came over the crater's rim in a green wave and stopped,
roots in the grey stone, at the edge of Morwen's salt; the sea drew back from the Drowned Coast; the snow lifted off the moors of Frostfall; the bells
of Aubade began to ring by themselves. Kessa's chains drew tight round the Tower's foot and sang. The Hundred Doors heaved against Wick's back. The
Tower's shadow, which had lain on the Ring for a hundred years, pulled at Big Yoko's feet like a tide, and he set them, and did not move.

And at the master's right hand, in the place where she had stood a hundred years before, Mum sang.

## The last verse

It was the first sound she had made since the Fall. Not a word, not a note, not a laugh, not a cry, for a hundred years; and then the first line of
the last verse, in the old tongue of Senmere, in the purest voice in the history of Aubade, a hundred years out of practice and a hair behind the beat.

[Cantor](../heroes/cantor.md), at the master's left, came in on the beat, under her, as if he had always known it, because he had been listening to her
for a hundred years and knew where she would go.

The master heard it.

He had been standing with his breath drawn for the last verse for a hundred years, and he let it out, and sang. The verse is a verse of evening and of
letting go, and a kept hour cannot hold someone who is singing it. Down the rows of the choir it went like a wind through grass: the ninety-eight woke
into the verse, one after another, the steadiest tenor in the hundred among them, on the beat, and Auriel, standing in front of him, heard her father's
voice for the first time in her life, and he saw her, and did not know her, and knew her.

Far off, faint, from the east, the bells of Aubade answered: a hundred bells, ringing on the beat, and for the first time in a hundred years none of them
short. [Campana](../heroes/campana.md) had stayed in the bell tower to ring the hundredth herself.

## The word taken

Iselm stood at his dial with his hands flat on it, as he had been kneeling on the day of the Fall, and the word that was his name strained in him
like a rope. He could feel the Aumbry fighting the verse. It had been made to keep; the verse was the only thing that had ever made it let go, and it
was being sung, by the voice it had waited ten years at an open gate to catch, and it was angry, in the patient, enormous way of a made thing that does
not know what anger is.

He did the one thing the word allowed him. He said his own name, aloud, as you say a name to someone who has asked for it.

"Iselm."

[Kohaku](../heroes/kohaku.md) took it out of the air as he said it, the way she takes every spell said aloud near her. For a moment she held in her two
hands the word that had moved the Aumbry across the dark between the stars, and it was the heaviest thing she had ever stolen, and she could not hold it
long. She threw it to her brother.

[The Great Zambini](../heroes/the-great-zambini.md) caught it in his hat.

He had played every court on the Dial and been thirty-nine for sixty years and was fading, slowly, in the empty hours between performances. He had always
wanted an audience at the Noon Seat: the whole Dial, watching, at once. He had it. Across the whole Dial, at noon on the Hundredth Midsummer, everyone was
looking up at the Tower, as Senmere had once looked up at its sun, because the Tear had opened over it and the sky was falling in; and what the whole Dial
saw, at the very top, small and clear against the gold, was a figure in a violet cape and a tall hat with a gold star, who bowed, and showed them his hat,
empty, and put it on his head.

The Aumbry had no word.

## The fall

For one breath, everything hung. The Tower did not keep and did not let go. The floors did not move. The Tear stopped widening. The ninety-eight singers
sang the last verse and the kept noon over the Seat neither held nor fell, and a mile below, in the room under the Heart, the old word on the wall went
pale and empty, like a page, and Hesk raised his chisel.

He had nothing to cut.

At the Sun Court's edge [Pip](../heroes/pip.md) the courier stood with Shiori's page in one hand and the Arbiter's horn in the other, and the Truce's
challenge delivered (he had given it to Iselm at dawn, by hand, and Iselm had read it, and signed it received, which was the only answer the word allowed).
[Zephyr](../heroes/zephyr.md) stood on the Noon Seat beside him, as he had always wanted, for one heartbeat.

Then they jumped.

It is a mile from the Sun Court to the Ring, straight down the Tower's grey face, and Zephyr's wind took them for most of it, a gust for every dash, the
two fastest people on the Dial racing each other down the outside of the Tower through the cloud with the page between them, and Lian's paper dragon diving
after them like a falling kite. [Pim](../heroes/pim-and-pom.md) slid down the outside behind them on a fold of the dragon's tail, shrieking with joy, as he
had always wanted to; Pom stayed at the top and laughed. At the last moment Zephyr's wind ran out and the dragon caught them both and dropped them on the
Ring's sand in front of Big Yoko, who caught Pip in one hand, and set him on his feet, and said, "Run."

Pip ran. Across the Ring, down the west tunnel, through the hall of Noonfall past Ash and Ember, down the stair into the Hall of Noon past a knight sitting
on the floor beside the Heart, down the Underforge's new shaft, into the room under the Tower, where a golem-maker stood with a chisel raised in front of a
blank wall. He put Shiori's page into Hesk's hand.

"Sign here," said Pip, out of breath. "And here. And there, where it's on fire."

Hesk looked at the word in Shiori's one stroke, in the script of Senmere, and cut it into the stone where the old word had been, in her hand, as the word
on every golem's brow is in her hand.

*Remember.*

At the top, Mum sang the last line of the last verse, and the Aumbry obeyed the last word written on it.

## The hand opens

It did not let go of everything at once. A keeper that remembers lets go the way an old man opens a box of letters: carefully, one at a time, reading each.

It let go of the noon. Over the Noon Seat the held gold moved, for the first time in a hundred years, like a breath let out. Up through the Tower from the
Heart, a mile below, came the Heart's light, undrunk for the first time since the Fall, all of it, the way heat comes up a flue, with the Ember of Aubade
burning in the middle of it like a seed in a fruit; and up the same flue, out of the hall of Noonfall, came Ash and Ember, the sun's last sparks, roaring.
"Everything that fell," they had said. "Bring the rest." [Anka](../heroes/anka.md) stood on the Noon Seat under the moving gold and asked them one last
time whether an Ember could choose to go home.

"Yes," they said. "Now."

She stepped into the noon. Ash and Ember met her in it, and the Heart's light rose into it, and the Ember of Aubade, and for a long moment the Noon Seat
was too bright to look at. [Silvane](../heroes/silvane.md), who had come up the last stair with her sister, opened the hand mirror in which she had caught
the sun's last light when she was twelve, and let it out: the sun's last look, which had been looking at her for a hundred years, went back to where it
belonged. [Opaline](../heroes/opaline.md) held up a crystal from the bottom of the Prism Stair with a colour in it that has no name, the colour the sun had
lost, and gave it back.

When the light could be looked at again, there was a sun over the Tower.

It was small, and young, and gold, and it was moving. It rose off the Noon Seat as the Ember of Aubade had once come down the Dawn Road, steadily, and stood
over the Tower at noon, and the Dial, looking up, felt it on their faces, warm, which nobody under sixty had ever felt. Sefu stood under it on the edge of the
Seat, where a king of the Sands had always said a king of the Sands would sit, and did not sit. He took the cursed crown off his head, and set it on the white
stone, on no head at all. Asenet, beside him, laughed for the first time in a hundred years. "It's yours now," she said. "It never stays." It stayed where he
had put it. Vesper stood in Nyx's shadow at the Seat's foot, out of the noon, and watched the sun rise that would burn her, and did nothing to stop it.

## Senmere's evening

It let go of Senmere last.

Through the window above the Seat the red sun of the first world it had ever kept began to move, after a thousand years at noon, and the terraces of
Senmere had their afternoon at last: long, and gold, and then red. The people on the terraces stopped being glad and still, and were glad and moving, and
then, as the light went down, tired, and then quiet, as people are at the end of a long day. Nerys at her window finished her question.

Iselm went up the last stair. He was very old now, older than anything on the Dial, and with his name gone he was nothing but a careful man who had made
something out of grief. He looked back once at the company on the Sun Court, and at the new sun over the Tower, and at Mum, who had sung his verse. Then he
went through the window into Senmere's evening, and up through the terraces to the pale tower in the middle of the city, and sat down on the edge of his
daughter's bed as the red sun went down, and answered her.

The Unmaker followed him. It was the evening of Senmere, and Senmere was having its evening now; it had somewhere to be at last. [Oblivion](../heroes/oblivion.md)
stopped at the window and looked back at Wick's Dial, the doors and the hours it had fought for a hundred years, and said, "There was something here," and
then, because it had learned the Dial's tongue well, "Thank you." Then it went through into the dusk of its own world, and was the dusk, and ended, as
everything ends.

Above the Tower the Tear began to close. [Thessa](../heroes/thessa.md) the Sky-Stitcher stood at the Seat's edge, nearer to it than anyone had ever been, and
sewed: two edges of the sky drawn together with a golden thread, stitch by stitch. On the other side, small careful fingers held the edges for her this time.
The children of Senmere had learned one more game. The last stitch went in at dusk, Senmere's dusk, and the dark between the stars was gone, and the sky over
the Tower was only sky.

Under the Ring, the Hundred Doors stopped pushing. Wick felt the largest door on the Dial close behind him, gently, and the lamp in front of the hundredth went
out on its own, for the first time in ninety-six years, because it was no longer needed.

## The shadow moves

In the Ring, in the straw-rope circle, with the new sun over the Tower and forty thousand people on their feet, Big Yoko looked down at the shadow across his
feet. It was not lying still any more. It was trembling, like a dog that wants to go.

He had stood in the circle since the Fall. He had always believed that if he stepped out, the shadow would move. He had stood there through a war and a hundred
years of the Truce, and lost once, and told nobody, and been very patient. He would have liked, he had told the company, for whoever climbed the Tower to come
back down and step out of the ring when it was time.

He stepped out.

The Tower's shadow moved. For the first time in a hundred years it left the Ring and went sweeping out across the crater, round the Dial, like the hand of a
clock, telling every realm its hour; and every realm, for the first time in a hundred years, agreed what time it was.
