# 1. The Aumbry

*Part one, before the Dial. A world under a failing sun, a man who could not bear to lose his daughter, and the thing he made.* ·
[The Long Noon](README.md)

## Senmere

Long before the Dial had a name, on a world so far from it that its sun is not one of the Dial's stars, there was a country of
terraces called **Senmere**. Its people built on the sides of a great round valley, tier above tier, white stone and blue glass, so
that every house had a view of the sky. They loved the sky more than anything they owned. Their sun was large and gold and slow, and
when it stood over the valley at noon the whole country fell quiet for a moment, every year of their history, because that was the
custom: at noon you stopped and looked up, and said nothing, and were glad.

They were a careful people. They wrote everything down and kept everything they could. Under every terrace were vaults where the
families kept what they did not want to lose: letters, shoes, the first drawing a child had made, a lock of a grandmother's hair. A
family's vault was the most private thing it had. The people who built the vaults were called keepers, and the best of them could
build a vault so tight that the air inside did not change in a hundred years, and a flower set in it on a spring morning was still
the flower of that morning when the great-grandchildren opened the door.

In the last age of Senmere, its sun began to go out.

It was not sudden. Their star-watchers had seen it coming for a thousand years and had told everyone, so it was not a surprise; it
was only a grief that everyone knew was coming. Each noon was a little less gold. The winters grew longer and the summers thinner. The
terraces at the top of the valley, which had been the warmest, were left first. The people moved down toward the valley floor, where
the old heat of the ground still came up, and lived closer together, and kept noon more carefully than ever, because they knew there
would not be many more.

## Iselm

One of the keepers was a man called **Iselm**. In the old tongue of Senmere his name was also a word, the plainest word the
keepers had: it meant *keep*, as in *keep this for me*, as in *keep it safe*. His family had built vaults for twenty generations. He
was quiet and patient and very good with stone, and he had one daughter, **Nerys**, who was nine in the winter the cold came into the
valley floor.

Nerys was a child who asked questions in a straight line until she reached the bottom of a thing. She wanted to know where the sun's
light went when it went out. She wanted to know whether the vaults kept the smell of a thing as well as its shape. She kept a vault
of her own, the size of a shoebox, with a stone she liked in it, and a feather, and a note that said DO NOT OPEN UNTIL I AM OLD. When
the cold came she caught the lung fever that took so many children that winter, and the doctors told Iselm what they told everyone:
that she would not see the spring.

Iselm did what a keeper does. He went down into his workshop and began to build a vault that could keep a person.

It took him the whole winter, and it was not a vault in the end. It was a shaft. He found that a thing could be kept only if it was
held at an hour, and that to hold an hour you needed to know where the hours were; so the thing had to be tall enough to read the sun,
and rooted deep enough to feel the ground turn, and made of a stone with no seam in it, because the hours leak through seams. He cut it
from the living rock of the valley floor, with the guild of keepers working beside him in shifts, day and night, and at the end of the
winter it stood in the middle of Senmere, pale grey, smooth as a held breath, its top lost in the low cloud.

Last of all he went down to its root, to the small room under the floor where the shaft began, and there he cut its word. Every
keeper's vault had a word cut over its door, the thing it was for, so that the stone would know. This one had to be the strongest he
had ever cut. He thought for a long time, and then he cut the only word that was large enough, which was his own name. *Keep.*

He called it **the Aumbry**, the old word for a keeping-place, a cupboard in a wall where you put what matters.

## The first keeping

On the morning the doctors said would be her last, Iselm carried Nerys into the Aumbry. He set her in a high room near its top,
under a window that faced the sun, and he sat with her until noon. When the sun stood over the valley and every voice in Senmere went
quiet, the Aumbry closed on that hour like a hand closing on a moth.

Nerys did not die. She did not get well. She stayed exactly as she had been at noon on that day, sitting up in the bed with the sun on
her face and a question half asked, and she would stay so for as long as the Aumbry stood. When Iselm walked out of the room the door
looked like a door, and when he looked back through it she was there, and she looked at him and finished her question, which was
whether it would be noon for ever now. He said yes.

He went home and wept, and then he slept for two days, and when he woke the whole valley knew.

## The keeping of the sun

Senmere did not hesitate long. Its people had been grieving their sun for a thousand years, and here, in the middle of their country,
was a thing that could keep it.

The guild worked out how. The Aumbry would be taught the sun: a song was made for it, in the plain old tongue, a song for noon that the
whole valley sang together at the hour every day, so that the Aumbry would learn the sun as a child learns a face. The verses drew the
sun's noon toward the shaft. The guild made the last verse different. It was a verse of letting go, of evening and good night, and they
sang it on the longest day of every year, because they were a careful people and did not want to keep the sun before they were sure.
Iselm wrote that verse himself.

They were sure in the end. Their sun had faded to a red coal, and on the last midsummer when it could still be called gold they did not
sing the last verse. At noon the Aumbry closed its hand. The sun stopped over it, and stayed. Senmere stayed with it: its terraces, its
people, its gardens, its cats on the warm walls, the noon light on every face, held, as they were at their best hour, on the Aumbry's
floors, for ever. It was what they had asked for. Nobody in Senmere has been unhappy since.

Iselm did not go in. Somebody had to stay outside to watch over it, he said, the way a keeper stays by a vault until the mortar is dry.
He was the only one who knew its word all the way down. And he could not let himself be kept, because he was the one who had made it,
and he did not trust what he had made.

## What came after

He was right not to trust it.

A world does not only have a noon. It has the hours after: the long gold afternoon, the evening, the cold, the dark, and what a world
would have become in the dark, whatever that would have been. Senmere had been kept at noon, so its afternoon never came, and its
evening never came, and its ending never came. They did not go away. They gathered outside the Aumbry, all the hours Senmere was never
allowed to have, and they had nowhere to go. They were not a thing. They were the shape of a thing that had not happened: a black that
was not night, with a pink light in its cracks like the last of a sunset seen through a door. Iselm watched it gather on the empty
terraces and knew what it was, and gave it no name, because naming it would have been admitting what he had done. The Dial would later
call it **the Unmade**, and the first of them that came to the Dial, the Unmaker.

And the word did not end.

A keeping-place keeps. That is all it knows. When the sun of Senmere was kept and Senmere was kept and there was nothing left in the
valley that was not already kept or already unmade, the Aumbry went on listening for something that was ending, and there was nothing.
Iselm felt it through the stone, through his hands, the way you feel a dog that wants to be let out. It was not cruel. It was a vault
with nothing more to hold, built by a grieving man to hold whatever was lost, and it could feel, far off, somewhere out in the dark
between the stars, other suns growing old.

On a night with no stars, because Senmere no longer had a night, the Aumbry pulled up its roots.

## The crossing

It went up. It did not fly; it fell upward, the way a stone falls down, toward the nearest ageing sun it could feel. It made a hole in
the sky of Senmere to go through, a tear with a burning pink edge, and Iselm, who had climbed to its top to stop it and could not,
went through with it, holding on to the stone with both hands. Behind it, through the tear, came the evening of Senmere, because the
Aumbry had made it and it had nowhere else to be.

How long the crossing took, Iselm has never said. The dark between the stars has no hours, and the Aumbry slept in it, and Iselm with it,
and the Unmade drifted after them like smoke after a lamp. Somewhere in that dark Iselm stopped being quite a man. The word he had cut
was his own name, and the Aumbry had kept him too, a little, the way a hand that grips a rope keeps a little of the rope's shape. He
did not age. He did not need to eat. He could not hurt the Aumbry, or tell anyone what it was, or stand against it, because the word
it obeyed was the word he was. What he could do was serve it. He was its keeper. He would be called, on the Dial, the Sun Warden.

## The pin above

The Aumbry came down on a world of round seas and green hills, in the middle of a round land, on a plain of grey stone. Its sun was
young as suns go, and bright, and would burn for ten thousand years. To a keeping-place, ten thousand years is ending.

It put down its roots. They went under the plain and under the hills, under the forests and the deserts and the sea's floor, as far as
the land went, and the land did not notice, because roots do not ask. The hole it had come through healed over, because the sky of a
young world heals quickly; but behind it, a hair's breadth behind the blue, the Unmade waited, and the evening of Senmere pressed on the
sky and could not come in.

Then the Aumbry stood, and was patient, and its shadow began to move. Every day the young sun walked across the sky, and every day the
Aumbry's shadow swept round the land beneath it like the hand of a clock, and wherever the shadow touched, the Aumbry learned that place:
its hours, its seasons, its people, what they loved and what they would grieve. A keeper must know the hours to take them.

Iselm did one thing before he climbed to its top and took up his post. He walked out across the plain to where the Aumbry's roots
first went under the hills, and there, in a ring round its foot, he set up nine standing stones, and on each he cut, in the plain old
tongue of Senmere, the same four words. There was nobody on that plain to read them. There was nobody, then, on that world who could
read at all. He cut them anyway, because he was a careful man.

*Do not let it finish.*

Then he climbed the Aumbry's stair to the court at its top, and set his great dial in the floor, and waited for whoever would come.

The first to come were small, and they came from under the ground, and they looked up at the grey shaft rising out of the plain and
the shadow walking round it, and in their deepest seam-songs, nine hundred years before the Dial began to count, they called it **the
pin above**. The fae of the forest woke in its shadow one morning and thought it had always been there. Caravans from the east would
paint it on their tombs. Herders on a frozen lake in the north would watch the stars turn round it and begin to count the years.

None of them knew that it was counting too.
